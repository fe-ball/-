# V23 REPLAY CHRONOLOGY — 1945 TO GENERAL ARMISTICE

**Status:** WORKING-CLOSED chronology spine  
**Guard:** Dates and loss figures not explicitly fixed in the decision register remain working bands. Do not treat this file as an archival census.

## January 1945 — Soviet offensive, German active defense

The Red Army retains the ability to produce overwhelming local artillery concentration and to break tactical defensive zones. It does not lose operational-art competence.

The differences from historical January 1945 are:

- smaller/compressed Vistula bridgeheads in several sectors;
- larger surviving German mobile reserves;
- materially stronger German aerial reconnaissance and operational aviation;
- no historical western destruction of German fuel/rail/training;
- higher accumulated Soviet losses and a thinner experienced mobile/second-echelon pool.

Soviet tank-army commitment is therefore more staged. German armored reserves are kept deeper than the historical shallow emergency placements and are used against extended mobile echelons rather than fed directly into the artillery breakthrough.

The Soviet advance continues, but no historical-form Vistula-Oder collapse is imported.

## February 1945 — repeated counterstroke, diminishing returns

Germany can repeat a limited operational counterstroke against a seam or extended mobile grouping.

The operational aim is not territorial reconquest. It is to force Soviet front-level mobile reserves to turn inward, protect flanks and spend repair/crew/command resources instead of continuing a deep exploitation.

The second use remains effective, but Soviet adaptation improves:

- better flank guards;
- tighter artillery follow-up;
- more cautious tank-army spacing;
- greater night movement/maskirovka;
- increased attention to German jet/ISR patterns.

German success therefore becomes more expensive.

## March 1945 — rebuild and qualitative accounting

Both sides rebuild.

Germany replaces vehicles more easily than experienced infantry, Panzergrenadiers and junior leaders.

The USSR replaces tanks and aircraft more easily than veteran tank crews, commanders, pilots/flight leaders, engineers, signals and maintainers.

The operational question shifts from raw inventory to the number of high-quality mobile operations that can still be executed and repeated.

## April 1945 — Soviet-Japanese treaty gate

The Soviet-Japanese Neutrality Pact remains in force.

Neither party denounces by 1945-04-25.

The pact auto-extends under the route center.

Moscow gains some freedom to release additional Far-East manpower/specialists westward, but Japan remains too strong for the Far East to be stripped of modern aviation, AA, mobile reserve and key technical infrastructure.

Japan gains legal/security time without entering the war.

## Spring 1945 — German limited offensive

Before newly released Soviet qualitative reinforcement fully changes the balance, Germany can still mount a limited spring offensive/counterstroke.

It demonstrates that Germany retains initiative at a selected operational point, but also demonstrates Germany's own limiting resource: experienced infantry/cadre cannot be regenerated as quickly as tanks and aircraft.

This does not reopen a fantasy strategic eastward conquest.

## Summer 1945 — Soviet shift to Romania/Ploiesti

Stavka adapts.

Rather than repeatedly maximizing deep races through central Poland into prepared German mobile defense, Soviet strategy emphasizes the Axis resource/alliance system.

Romania/Ploiesti becomes the major strategic target.

Soviet attack can break tactical zones, but Germany has had more preparation time and withdraws major German formations earlier than the historical 1944 collapse.

Romania nevertheless reaches a political exit point.

Ploiesti leaves the German economic system.

## Late summer / autumn 1945 — fuel flexibility and Hungary

Ploiesti loss is not an immediate German fuel-system death because:

- synthetic-fuel plants have not suffered the historical US Oil Campaign;
- German transport and stock management are healthier;
- Germany retains other fuel sources.

The effect is progressive loss of flexibility:

- fewer discretionary theater-to-theater mobile moves;
- lower non-priority transport/sortie/training margins;
- increased importance of Hungarian/Austrian/domestic fuel sources;
- greater need to prioritize Luftwaffe and mobile-reserve fuel.

Hungary becomes the next political/military crisis.

Germany prevents an immediate Romanian-style army-wide flip by intervening in the Hungarian political command structure while retaining enough Hungarian military bureaucracy to keep the front functioning.

The price is occupation/security manpower and reduced allied reliability.

## Late 1945 — technology

Germany still fields a coherent late-war military.

Mature systems include the conventional fighter/attack backbone plus meaningful Me 262/E6-D/Ar 234 operations.

Type XXI moves from historical premature/end-of-war status toward a real operational fleet because production, rail, yard, training and fuel systems have not collapsed in the same way.

HeS 011-class development may reach low-rate/service-development state. This does not authorize mass speculative next-generation aircraft fleets.

Soviet armored and piston-aircraft systems remain formidable. New-generation tanks can appear in limited/evaluation use. A mass mature Soviet jet ecosystem is not assumed simply because German jets exist.

## Early 1946 — political objective changes

Germany's realistic political objective narrows.

It no longer needs to preserve the non-German continental empire to claim survival.

The essential objectives become:

- German homeland not comprehensively occupied;
- central German state continuity;
- a reduced national military;
- end of the war before cumulative cadre/alliance/fuel erosion becomes terminal.

This makes armistice exploration compatible with a German internal narrative of defensive survival rather than unconditional surrender.

## Armistice feelers

Berlin uses separate real diplomatic channels for separate audiences while keeping the substantive offer general/all-front:

- Stockholm/Sweden toward Britain;
- Bern/Switzerland for confidential multilateral contact;
- Tokyo/Japanese diplomatic channels as one plausible route to Moscow.

The first German position does not offer enough territorial/military concession.

The existence of a concrete Allied/Soviet reply nevertheless shows Berlin that negotiation is possible.

## Second position / formal talks

Germany accepts:

- withdrawal from non-German occupied areas;
- restoration/separation of several occupied European states;
- major force reductions and inspection;
- stopping U-boat and long-range strike campaigns;
- humanitarian access and detainee/forced-labor handling;
- later reparations and political/legal negotiations.

The other side does not need to recognize or permanently guarantee the Nazi political system merely to sign a military armistice with the effective German government.

## May–June 1946 — General European Armistice

A general armistice enters force in this time class.

Germany is not comprehensively occupied and is not immediately deprived of all national armed force.

The non-German occupation empire is dismantled.

Final peace issues are deferred.

This is the V23 historical stop line.
