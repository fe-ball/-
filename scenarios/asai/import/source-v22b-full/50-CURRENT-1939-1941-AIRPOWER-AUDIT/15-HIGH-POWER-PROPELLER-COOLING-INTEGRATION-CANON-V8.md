# HIGH-POWER PROPELLER / COOLING INTEGRATION CANON — V8 AIRPOWER4

> **AIRPOWER4 current high-power aircraft installation authority.** Engine power that cannot be absorbed by the propeller or cooled inside the actual cowling is not aircraft power. This file prevents the engine-only audit from being converted directly into aircraft speed.

## 0. Core decision

For piston aircraft above roughly the 1,500–2,000 hp class, the aircraft program must close three semi-independent systems:

1. **propeller absorption/control**;
2. **engine/gear/shaft vibration**;
3. **cooling/oil/cowling/exhaust integration**.

A mature engine does not close them automatically.

## 1. Propeller state

Required items include:

- blade count;
- diameter;
- reduction ratio;
- blade section/twist;
- constant-speed/governor behavior;
- overspeed/transient control;
- spinner/hub;
- ground/gear clearance;
- tip Mach/compressibility;
- structural fatigue;
- production balance/repeatability.

The same engine may require different propellers for fighter, bomber or naval installation.

## 2. Four-blade and high-power development

Worldline Japan can investigate four-blade/high-power propellers before historical mass adoption because:

- Asai balancing/vibration/metrology methods are available;
- high-power turbine/free-spool programs provide rotational test culture;
- later engine requirements are visible as power density rises.

But a usable four-blade propeller still requires physical hub/blade/governor testing.

Therefore by 1941–42 a serious high-power piston program may possess PA-D/PA-Q combinations earlier, but not every aircraft automatically gets a perfect four-blade propeller.

## 3. Cooling integration

Air-cooled radial installation must resolve:

- cylinder temperature distribution;
- baffles/seals;
- cowl pressure recovery;
- exit area;
- oil cooler;
- ground/taxi cooling;
- sustained climb;
- hot day;
- high-speed pressure;
- exhaust heat interaction.

Do not infer cooling from engine bench rating.

## 4. Oil cooling

High power may be oil-system limited before cylinder-head limited. Check:

- oil temperature;
- cooler drag;
- flow and pressure;
- scavenge/aeration;
- ground operation;
- altitude.

## 5. Liquid-cooled radiator installations

For Ki-61/D4Y-like designs, radiator duct performance is a separate major aerodynamic card.

A well-designed duct may recover part of cooling drag/heat through momentum effect, but it requires:

- inlet diffuser;
- core pressure loss;
- exit/nozzle geometry;
- mass flow;
- temperature rise;
- speed condition;
- controllable flap/exit;
- structural/thermal installation.

Do not assume “Meredith effect” as a free fixed speed bonus.

## 6. Exhaust interaction

EX3 exhaust design includes cooling-exit interaction. Avoid double counting an exhaust speed gain and a cooling-drag gain that arise from the same cowling pressure-field change.

## 7. Carrier aircraft

Carrier types add:

- deck/gear/propeller clearance;
- low-speed thrust;
- go-around response;
- folding/spotting if relevant;
- arresting/landing structural loads.

A very large high-power propeller may be unacceptable despite better climb/speed.

## 8. Qualification states

Use PA-D/PA-Q/PA-S and CI-D/CI-Q/CI-S where useful.

An aircraft can fly with a development propeller/cooling installation before either is production ready.

## 9. Aircraft-performance guard

When using engine output in aircraft calculations, state:

- shaft power at altitude;
- reduction/prop efficiency assumption;
- exhaust thrust separately if applicable;
- cooling drag state;
- aircraft weight/configuration;
- whether value is development or production.

Never multiply max engine gain, max exhaust gain, max cooling gain and max material saving independently.
