Operational translation:

- Asai-exposed heavy-transport pool: +10–18% ready-ton-km

- destroyed bridges, deep mud, port congestion and route denial can erase most of the advantage

## Nanjing regime effect

By summer 1940, Wang Jingwei’s Nanjing regime begins to matter in rear areas through:

- police / local security
- taxation / labor / warehouse administration
- railway/river-station support
- information and local bureaucracy
- partial replacement of Japanese/static manpower in lower-risk tasks

It is **not** treated as an extra Japanese field division and does not receive a separate +X% logistics scalar.

The effect is already contained in the state of the lower-Yangtze rear system: Japanese specialists and transport assets can be shifted somewhat more easily to emergencies elsewhere.

## Railway sabotage / Hundred Regiments pre-rule

Railway destruction is split into four layers:

1. **bulk transport** — coal, fuel, mass ammunition, food: railway break still hurts strongly.
2. **general military supply** — partly bypassable by railhead-to-railhead truck shuttle.
3. **priority cargo** — precision parts, radio/crypto, medical items, specialist personnel: can remain mobile by truck and, for very light/high-value loads, E6 Twin.
4. **command continuity** — radio, courier aircraft and redundant staff links prevent a line break from automatically paralyzing command.

Therefore small track cuts increasingly shrink toward “the explosion itself,” while bridges, tunnels, yards, mines, power and repair nodes remain strategically serious.

Do not reduce physical sabotage automatically. Instead modify:

- average outage duration
- priority-cargo delay
- command disruption
- repair-team dispatch speed
- alternate-road / river use

Security response may include radio patrol cars, heavy 6x4 response teams, armored cars, rail motorcars, improved armored-train availability, engineer/repair columns and mobile GT power/lighting. These are improved security/support assets, not a new mechanized army.
