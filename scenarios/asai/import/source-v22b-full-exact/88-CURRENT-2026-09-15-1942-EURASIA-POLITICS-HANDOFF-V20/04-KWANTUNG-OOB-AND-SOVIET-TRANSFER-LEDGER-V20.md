## 2. Japanese theater disposition logic

### East / Ussuri-Primorye — main offensive concept

1941 working structure:
- 3rd Army: 4 divisions;
- 5th Army: 3 divisions;
- both armored groupings concentrated mainly on the eastern front;
- most heavy artillery, high-quality tractors, new 47-mm systems and high-performance aviation are eastern-priority;
- 4-division-equivalent strategic reserve can move toward the east in days to roughly a week under prepared rail plans.

Operational intent:
- do not frontal-assault Vladivostok as the opening move;
- penetrate toward Ussuri/rail nodes;
- cut the Primorye connection and isolate Vladivostok;
- use Navy/air power for blockade/mines/ASW/port pressure;
- only later reduce the fortress/port.

### North / Amur

- smaller standing force;
- defensive first, conditional crossings if Soviet situation collapses;
- North Sakhalin is treated separately as an Army-Navy joint operation.

### West / Hailar-Manzhouli

- deliberately weaker and more defensive;
- main lesson from Nomonhan is to deny/attrit Soviet armored counterattack rather than launch an under-supported deep offensive;
- Soviet units from Transbaikal/inner sectors are therefore more extractable westward than Primorye units.

## 4. Soviet transfer rules

1. A unit already ordered/moving west before the Japanese threat changes cannot be silently restored.
2. Moscow does not choose between “all east” and “all west.” It extracts from less threatened sectors first.
3. Japanese eastern concentration disproportionately holds Ussuri/Primorye and coastal formations.
4. Japanese weak western posture allows more Transbaikal/inner-sector extraction.
5. New Soviet mobilization can replace headcount while still degrading the quality/mobility of eastern reserves; headcount alone is not the “thinness” metric.
6. Transfer effects should be evaluated by **order date, rail loading, theater arrival, combat-readiness and actual battle employment**.

## 6. Consequence guard

Do not state “Japan prevents X divisions from saving Moscow/Stalingrad, therefore Germany wins.”

The effect chain is narrower:
- some historical Soviet reinforcements are absent or delayed;
- specific Soviet defensive sectors become thinner;
- local German tactical/operational progress may improve;
- Soviet high command consumes other reserves earlier;
- German logistics/fuel/attrition still constrain exploitation;
- Japanese local odds do not automatically improve because the same retained Soviet units remain opposite Japan.

This two-sided effect is central to V20.
