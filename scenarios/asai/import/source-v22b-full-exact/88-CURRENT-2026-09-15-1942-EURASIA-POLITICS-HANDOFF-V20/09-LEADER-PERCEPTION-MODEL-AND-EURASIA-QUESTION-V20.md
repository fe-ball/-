# LEADER PERCEPTION MODEL / THE EURASIA QUESTION — NEXT POLITICAL HANDOFF

## 1. Purpose

This file is explicitly **not objective world truth**. It stores the actor-perception framework that should guide the next political discussion.

Every important claim should be classified as one of:
- FACT: replayed/observable world event;
- ESTIMATE: intelligence/community inference;
- BELIEF: a leader/faction's internal model;
- PLAN-INPUT: a number used by planners whether or not true;
- PUBLIC-LINE: propaganda/diplomatic language that may not equal private belief.

The next session should resist collapsing these categories.

## 2. The expanded German Question

By 1942 the traditional European “German Question” has become a Eurasian balance problem.

The mechanism is not rhetorical. It runs through military geography:
- German victories/defeats in the European plain determine Soviet demand for eastern formations;
- Soviet east-west rail transfers change the force facing Manchuria;
- Japanese mobilization changes which Soviet reserves can be sent to Europe;
- Finnish operations draw formations that otherwise might face Japan or Germany;
- a German victory can either remove the Soviet threat or push a surviving Soviet state eastward;
- a Soviet victory can eventually free an enormous battle-experienced Red Army for Asia;
- a German-Soviet peace can free both armies at once.

Thus a European continental outcome can directly determine the security of Manchuria, Sakhalin, Korea and the Japanese home islands.

## 3. Tokyo's recognized end-state menu

### Outcome A — mutual attrition / no decisive victor
Likely recognized widely. Privately attractive to Konoe/FM/Navy and tolerable to Army for a time.

### Outcome B — German western victory, Soviet remnant survives east
Recognizable and dangerous. Japan may face a Soviet state whose government/industry/army are compressed toward Siberia.

### Outcome C — Soviet collapse + Japanese Far-East faits accomplis
Army/Kwantung favorite. Requires either Japanese action or a Soviet state collapse so deep that local occupation is low-cost.

### Outcome D — Soviet collapse + German power extends east before Japan settles its sphere
Recognized as a real alliance-risk. Japan does not assume a victorious Germany will be permanently friendly or modest.

### Outcome E — German-Soviet negotiated peace
Recognized as dangerous: both armies can become available; Japan loses its force-fixation advantage.

### Outcome F — Soviet strategic recovery while Germany still pins most Red Army strength
Recognized as the most dangerous “last chance” window for preventive-war advocates.

### Outcome G — decisive Soviet victory
In 1942 this is less widely expected than A/B/C, but is recognizable. Army sees a future Manchurian threat; diplomats/naval strategists can also see an eventual US/UK interest in preventing unlimited Soviet Asian expansion.

## 4. Tokyo factional preference model

This is a working political model only.

### Emperor / Kido
- wants empire/homeland security and avoidance of an unwinnable general war;
- skeptical of short-war promises after China/USSR experience;
- likely tolerates A; can accept a controlled C; fears north->south->US war chain.

### Konoe
- likely sees A as close to optimal;
- fears either German or Soviet domination of Eurasia;
- may overestimate diplomacy's ability to preserve flexibility indefinitely.

### Toyoda / Foreign Ministry
- favors trade + neutrality + bargaining;
- wants postwar boundaries clarified before Japan sacrifices neutrality;
- likely rates US/UK political reaction more seriously than Army planners.

### Tojo / Army Ministry
- places high value on Manchurian security;
- can support C and possibly N-PREV;
- not necessarily identical to “attack immediately”; needs a usable war aim and state decision.

### Sugiyama / General Staff
- seeks a calculable limited Soviet campaign: Primorye isolation, North Sakhalin, reduction of Far-East threat;
- greatest analytic danger is treating accurate computation with uncertain inputs as accurate prediction.

### Umezu / Kwantung
- likely higher confidence in improved Japanese readiness/logistics;
- may correctly know that 1942 Kwantung is far stronger than 1939 while over-extrapolating Manchurian exercise logistics into Soviet territory.

### Navy Ministry / Naval General Staff
- central question is not “can Army beat Soviets locally?” but “does Soviet war trigger embargo and therefore force southern/global war?”
- US carrier-building clock also makes infinite waiting unattractive.

### Yamamoto-type view
- sees US long-war problem as fundamental;
- likely most hostile to a policy that turns one Soviet war into a second southern war through sanctions.

### Oshima/Berlin channel
- most exposed to German optimism and most likely to see C as achievable now;
- can sincerely believe Germany is near decisive success even after Tokyo's broader system becomes more skeptical.

## 5. Berlin's likely conceptual problem — OPEN

Germany knows Japan is being asked to take a major risk and that Japan now wants a clearer post-Soviet settlement. What is **not yet closed**:
- does Hitler/Ribbentrop take Japanese Far-East boundary demands seriously?
- does Berlin envision a continuing Russian/Siberian state, direct German influence, local collaboration regimes, or vague spheres?
- does Germany think Japan should attack first and bargain later?
- how much does Berlin understand that an eastward-compressed Soviet remnant is a Japanese nightmare rather than a German victory gift?
- does Germany recognize that Japanese non-entry also keeps the United States formally outside the war?

This is a highest-priority next political audit.

## 6. Moscow's likely conceptual problem — OPEN

Moscow must balance:
- need for western reinforcements;
- need to deter Japan;
- value of Neutrality Pact and North Sakhalin/fisheries concessions;
- Pacific Lend-Lease access;
- fear that appearing too weak invites Japanese opportunism;
- fear that appearing too strong encourages preventive Japanese war.

Question: does Stalin see a controlled Japanese neutrality as a purchasable strategic asset, and how much is he willing to pay for it?

## 7. London/Washington conceptual problem — OPEN

They can simultaneously believe:
- Japan is an Axis treaty partner and latent aggressor;
- Japan's attack on USSR would be disastrous;
- over-pressure could create exactly that attack or a southern war;
- a nonbelligerent Japan leaves British Asian possessions intact;
- in a future Soviet-victory world, Japan might become a useful East-Asian balance — but China/Manchuria questions make any rapprochement politically difficult.

The critical uncertainty is **trigger knowledge**: Tokyo does not know the real US/UK response function, and Washington/London do not publish one.

A Roosevelt speech promising non-entry under one context can be misused in Tokyo as “proof” that America will not enter a different war. Conversely, a harsh public speech can be treated as proof of inevitable embargo even when the administration intends only deterrence.

## 8. Chongqing conceptual problem — OPEN

Chongqing benefits from Japanese withdrawal but does not benefit from Japan becoming dramatically stronger through Siberian gains.

Possible preference:
- Japan out of China;
- no Soviet/Japanese collapse that lets the other dominate Northeast Asia;
- continued access to US/UK support;
- enough Soviet survival to constrain Japan, but not so much Soviet/CCP strength that postwar Chinese politics becomes impossible.

This makes Chinese diplomacy part of the Eurasian balance question, not only a bilateral peace issue.

## 9. Next-session checklist

Audit each capital separately:
1. what end-states can it imagine?
2. which does it prefer privately?
3. which can it say publicly?
4. what information is its judgment based on?
5. what does it think the other powers' trigger lines are?
6. where is it most likely to misread ambiguous speeches/diplomatic language?
7. what concrete offer or threat can it make now?
8. what future result would make it reverse policy?
