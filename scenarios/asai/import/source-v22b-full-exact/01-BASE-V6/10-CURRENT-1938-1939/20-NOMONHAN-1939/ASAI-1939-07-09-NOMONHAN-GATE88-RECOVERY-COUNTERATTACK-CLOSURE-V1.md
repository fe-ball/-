## 0. Historical anchors retained

- 4 July: generally clear; Soviet mechanized reinforcement reaches the sector.
- 5 July: rain and deep cloud materially degrade observation.
- 6 July: generally clear; Soviet counterattack includes a large armored/motorized component. Japanese 4th Tank Regiment reporting records roughly 75 tanks, 800 truck-borne infantry, and an additional rifle regiment in the counterattack picture.
- 7/8 July: Japanese night infantry attack after a short artillery preparation takes some ground but does not expel Soviet forces from the east bank.
- Historical 9 July: Yasuoka Detachment is dissolved and the two tank regiments are ordered out after the failure of the armored offensive.

The worldline does not erase the Soviet reinforcement schedule, the Japanese west-bank failure, or the Soviet material superiority.

## 1. Tank recovery first: July 3 evening to July 6

Gate 87 closes July 2/3 Japanese armored damage at:
- 30–35 disabled/lost in the immediate battle accounting;
- 8–10 irrecoverable;
- therefore roughly 20–27 recoverable vehicles remain in the repair/recovery pool.

Worldline advantages already closed:
- workshop turnaround 15–25% shorter for routine/recoverable failures;
- better towing and recovery margin;
- 5–7 mobile GT utility sets in theater, with 2 concentrated on workshop/recovery tasks;
- stronger standardized tools, gauges and spares;
- better route/fuel support;
- no magical spare-parts abundance and no recovery under direct Soviet fire by fiat.

### July 4
Clear weather allows the strongest recovery day.
CLOSED:
- 8–11 disabled vehicles returned to running condition by 24:00;
- 2–4 additional hulls recovered to workshops but not yet combat-ready;
- recovery priority is mobility/fire-control/engine faults first, major armor/track/gearbox damage second.

### July 5
Rain and cloud slow towing, observation and outdoor repair.
CLOSED:
- only 3–5 additional vehicles return to combat readiness;
- workshop repair continues under GT-powered lighting/tools, so rain reduces field retrieval more than bench/workshop output;
- no aviation reconnaissance credit beyond local weather openings.

### July 6 pre-counterattack state
Central working state before Soviet pressure:
- approximately 50–55 Japanese tanks mechanically running;
- of these, about 46–51 are genuinely combat-ready after crew replacement, ammunition and fuel checks;
- remaining vehicles are reserve/recovery/crew-limited rather than all immediately usable.

This is materially stronger than matched history, but still below the original 73-tank establishment.

## 2. Soviet July 6 counterattack

Japanese 4th Tank Regiment reporting identifies a major Soviet armored/motorized counterattack picture on 6 July, after mechanized reinforcements arrived on 4 July.

Worldline reconnaissance effect:
- clear 6 July weather permits repeated Ki-15-II deep/medium sweeps;
- Soviet vehicle concentrations cannot be hidden entirely on open steppe;
- Japanese headquarters receives earlier warning of a large armored movement than in a single-sortie intelligence model;
- no perfect count or intent prediction is credited.

Ki-36 direct-cooperation detachment:
- 2 aircraft reach usable forward status on 6 July;
- full six-aircraft detachment is not yet ready;
- initial use is artillery observation / route and vehicle-column reporting, not offensive low-level attack.

## 3. Artillery and anti-armor response

The 6 July Soviet counterattack is the best early test of the worldline artillery-support package.

CLOSED:
- pre-registered likely approach corridors and vehicle assembly areas are used;
- Ki-15-II warning and the first Ki-36 observation improve the timing of Japanese fires;
- motorized/tractor artillery can shift some batteries 10–15% faster where tracks remain passable;
- dense-core Type 94 37 mm AT ammunition is issued selectively to the most exposed AT positions;
- Japanese artillery still suffers inferior absolute shell supply and shorter range than heavier Soviet systems.

Special 37 mm AT ammunition through 6 July:
- additional consumption: about 180–260 rounds;
- forward reserve remains sufficient for later July fighting but is no longer abundant at every gun.

CLOSED combat effect on 6 July:
- Soviet/Mongolian AFV additional mission-kills versus matched history: +6 to +10;
- a portion are earlier kills of vehicles that historical Japanese forces might have disabled later; pure net permanent-loss difference is smaller;
- Japanese line is not broken by the first armored rush.

## 4. Japanese armor use after the July 2/3 failure

Gate 87 lessons are not ignored.
Japanese tanks are no longer ordered to repeat an unsupported deep penetration on 4–6 July.

CLOSED doctrine-in-practice delta:
- tanks are held as a local mobile counterattack / anti-penetration reserve behind infantry and AT belts;
- platoon/company-sized armor supports local counterattacks only after artillery preparation or confirmed Soviet movement;
- no second Yasuoka-style mass charge toward the Halha is authorized in this gate.

This is not a general IJA doctrinal revolution. It is a local reaction to an extremely recent and costly battle report.

Soviet 45 mm guns, tanks, artillery and air attack remain lethal.
CLOSED additional Japanese armored damage during the 4–6 July fighting:
- 5–8 additional tanks temporarily disabled;
- 1–2 additional irrecoverable losses;
- 2–3 of the temporary losses are returned by the evening of 9 July.

Cumulative immediate irrecoverable tank losses through 9 July:
- about 9–12, central 10–11.

## 6. July 7/8 night infantry attack

Historical sequence retained: Japanese conduct a night infantry attack after an approximately 30-minute artillery preparation and gain some ground, but Soviet daylight counteraction prevents decisive expulsion from the east-bank bridgehead.

Worldline effects:
- prepared artillery is more repeatable and wastes somewhat fewer shells;
- GT-powered command/communications/workshop nodes reduce electrical interruptions;
- Ki-36 cannot observe at night, but on 8 July daylight it accelerates correction of Soviet gun/vehicle positions for subsequent fires;
- tanks remain mainly mobile reserve; a few vehicles support consolidation, not the first night assault wave.

CLOSED result:
- Japanese local penetration is somewhat cleaner and the captured strip is marginally deeper/wider in places;
- Soviet motorized infantry/artillery counterattacks still recover much of the exposed ground;
- no east-bank Soviet expulsion.

Japanese personnel loss avoided over 4–9 July versus matched history: about 90–140.
Soviet/Mongolian additional casualties/temporary combat losses from better artillery/AT integration: about 180–300.

## 7. July 9 Yasuoka decision gate

Historical trigger had two components:
1. the operational plan failed;
2. the armored regiments suffered sufficiently severe losses that Kwantung Army withdrew them and dissolved the detachment.

In the worldline, (1) remains true but (2) is materially weaker.

CLOSED command decision:
- **Yasuoka Detachment is still formally dissolved on 9 July.**
- Reason: the pincer operation has failed, west-bank forces have already withdrawn, and the ad hoc detachment is no longer the correct command structure.
- **Yasuoka is relieved from this failed task-force command.** The better tank result does not erase the failed operational objective.
- However, the historical automatic withdrawal of both tank regiments from the theater is NOT retained.

New disposition:
- infantry/artillery/engineer elements revert to normal divisional/army control;
- 3rd and 4th Tank Regiments are reconstituted as a Kwantung Army / 23rd Division-area armored reserve, not as an independent deep-penetration task force;
- badly damaged vehicles and nonessential maintenance echelons are sent rearward;
- combat-ready tank strength retained near the theater on 9 July: **48–53 tanks**, central about 50;
- mechanically running but crew/repair-limited additional vehicles: 3–5;
- total armor physically present in theater therefore remains above the immediate combat-ready count.

This is the main branch divergence of Gate 88.

## 8. Consequence for later July

The Japanese now enter the next phase with:
- roughly 50 combat-ready tanks still available as operational reserve;
- 9–12 irrecoverable tank losses instead of a force treated as effectively spent;
- stronger recovery/workshop infrastructure;
- Ki-36 artillery-observation capability reaching full six-aircraft detachment strength by 8–9 July;
- depleted but still meaningful dense-core 37 mm reserves;
- no solution to radio density, Soviet artillery mass, Soviet logistical scale, or Japanese shell-production limits.

Therefore the historical shift to night infantry attacks still occurs, but Japanese daytime defensive/counterattack capacity is stronger.
The next major question is whether retaining armor causes useful defensive resilience or encourages another over-aggressive local attack before the 23 July artillery offensive.
