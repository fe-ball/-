# ASAI 1939 — NOMONHAN
## Exchange ratios, Soviet reaction, and Japanese staff counterfactual
Status basis: post-31 August battle assessment; later archival-quality truth is kept separate from in-world estimates.

## 1. External adjudication: personnel exchange

Historical comparison anchor:
- Japanese combat casualties: roughly 17.4–17.7k depending accounting basis.
- Soviet combat casualties plus Mongolian casualties: roughly 25.9k.
- historical casualty-exchange ratio therefore about 1.46–1.49 Soviet/Mongolian casualties per Japanese combat casualty.
- historical fatal/dead-missing exchange is only about 1.15–1.18:1.

Branch adjudication:
- Japanese combat casualties: about 14.7–15.7k.
- Soviet/Mongolian combat casualties: about 26.6–27.1k.
- casualty-exchange ratio: **1.69–1.84:1**, central about **1.76:1**.
- Japanese dead/missing: about 6.4–7.0k.
- Soviet/Mongolian dead/missing/fatal: about 10.2–10.45k.
- fatality exchange: about **1.46–1.63:1**.

The branch improves the Japanese exchange rate mainly by:
1. avoiding several unnecessary attacks through better reconnaissance;
2. retaining artillery/AT/tank support longer;
3. reducing vehicle and recovery losses;
4. extracting organized cadres and wounded before/through encirclement;
5. inflicting additional local AFV/crew and artillery casualties.

This does not change the Soviet operational victory.

## 2. Aviation exchange

Historical combat-aircraft loss anchor:
- Soviet: about 208 combat losses.
- Japanese: about 162 combat losses/write-offs from battle damage.
- rough historical exchange: about 1.28:1.

Branch:
- Soviet combat losses adjudicated: about 212–218.
- Japanese combat losses/write-offs: about 150–156.
- branch combat-aircraft exchange: about **1.36–1.45:1**.

No blanket fighter accuracy bonus is used.
Improvement comes from:
- aircraft readiness;
- dispersal;
- shorter response time;
- reconnaissance quality;
- avoiding some ground destruction;
- better target preparation for bombing.

## 3. AFV exchange

Do not use a single "tank kill ratio" without qualification.
Most Soviet AFV losses were caused by Japanese AT guns, artillery, mines and infantry, not Japanese tanks.

Branch cumulative additional Soviet/Mongolian AFV **mission-kills** over the historical-style baseline:
approximately **+38 to +61 events**.
Some are repeat damage or vehicles later recovered by Soviet forces.

Adjudicated additional permanent Soviet/Mongolian AFV losses attributable to the branch:
approximately **+15 to +25 vehicles**.

Japanese branch permanent tank losses through 31 August:
approximately **18–24 tanks** as a planning band, with 30–36 tanks physically surviving/recoverable east of the battlefield.

Therefore:
- Japanese tank-vs-tank exchange is not claimed.
- Japanese **combined anti-armor system** remains highly cost-effective.
- the principal Soviet answer is artillery/air suppression and mass, not trying to duel Japanese AT guns one-for-one.

## 4. Soviet reaction

### Tactical
**HIGH concern.**
Soviet commanders observe:
- unusually persistent Japanese 37 mm AT effectiveness;
- more Japanese armor remaining in action after July than expected;
- faster Japanese vehicle recovery;
- repeated Japanese reconnaissance;
- better artillery displacement and first-round effectiveness;
- Japanese local counterattacks increasingly tied to AT/artillery rather than isolated tank charges.

Likely Soviet conclusion:
"Japanese infantry remains dangerous; its anti-tank defense and local mechanized reserve are stronger than expected. Do not attack prepared positions without artillery/air preparation."

### Operational
**MODERATE-HIGH concern, but no panic.**
The Red Army still proves it can defeat the Japanese system by:
- larger armored mass;
- long-range artillery;
- much larger ammunition expenditure;
- motor transport;
- air superiority;
- deception and operational envelopment.

The branch lesson for Soviet command is therefore not "Japan has caught us technologically."
It is:
"Japan is becoming more mechanically resilient; future operations require the full combined-arms package and should not assume rapid collapse after the first penetration."

### Technical intelligence
By September it is plausible that Soviet technical intelligence obtains at least one damaged/captured Japanese turbo-diesel engine or prime-mover installation.
The exhaust turbocharger itself is identifiable.
This raises interest in Japanese heavy-vehicle power and recovery capability.

A complete mobile GT utility set is not assumed captured.
Soviet field reports may note unusual compact power/utility equipment, but they do not yet infer the full scale of Asai gas-turbine industry.

### Strategic
**Concern increases one grade, deterrence does not flip.**
Moscow sees Japan as:
- tactically costly;
- improving in mechanization;
- still deficient in long-range artillery, logistics depth, radios, and mass motorization.

The desire to stabilize the Far Eastern front is strengthened, especially with the European war underway.
But the Soviet leadership does not conclude that the Kwantung Army can conduct a successful deep mechanized invasion of Mongolia/Siberia.

## 5. Japanese staff counterfactual: "enough turbo-diesels + enough Chi-Ha"

### Study rule
All other conditions remain unchanged:
- same Japanese manpower;
- same Soviet forces and decisions;
- same weather;
- same artillery types and shell production;
- same aircraft;
- same number of trucks/tractors;
- same bridges and water sources;
- same radios;
- same doctrine and command personalities;
- same 37 mm AT gun numbers and special-AP stock;
- same intelligence system as the actual branch.

Only two substitutions are made.

### Variable T — sufficient turbo-diesel fleet
By June:
- roughly 70–80% of heavy artillery prime movers, heavy 6x4 cargo trucks, recovery/workshop vehicles, and other high-load diesel vehicles are low-pressure turbocharged.
- vehicle count does not increase.

Expected technical effects:
- heavy-vehicle mechanical-ready fraction: roughly +8–12 points over historical-type fleet;
- passable-terrain heavy transport throughput: +10–15%;
- recovery/workshop turnaround: about 25–30% shorter;
- artillery displacement reliability/time: roughly 15–20% better.
No improvement is credited when terrain becomes physically impassable.

### Variable C — Chi-Ha saturation
The two tank regiments still field about 73 tanks.
Instead of a mixed force dominated by older/light types, the combat tank establishment is essentially Type 97 Chi-Ha.

No changes to:
- 57 mm low-velocity main gun;
- radio density;
- crew training;
- doctrine;
- ammunition quantity.

The 25 mm-class armor is better than the lighter vehicles it replaces but still does not stop the Soviet 45 mm AT/tank gun at normal battle ranges.

A low-pressure turbo-Chi-Ha subcase gives about 195–205 hp-class usable output rather than the standard roughly 170 hp-class rating.
The staff finds that this helps acceleration, soft-ground recovery and towing far more than it helps direct tank-versus-tank lethality.

## 6. Staff wargame result

### July 2–3
Japanese attack still fails operationally.
Reasons unchanged:
- Soviet 45 mm guns penetrate Chi-Ha;
- Japanese radios/coordination remain weak;
- 57 mm is not a strong anti-tank gun;
- Soviet artillery and armored mass remain large.

However:
- fewer mechanical stragglers;
- better HE support against AT-gun positions;
- stronger recovery.

Expected combat-ready tanks around 9 July:
**about 58–63**, rather than roughly 50 in the actual branch.

### July artillery phase
Turbo prime movers help materially.
But shell tonnage and gun range are unchanged.
The Japanese still lose the artillery contest.

### 20 August offensive
Available Japanese tank reserve:
approximately **60–66 combat-ready tanks**.

The Soviet southern wing is delayed further, but not stopped.
The staff estimate is that the operational ring would likely close roughly **12–24 hours later** than in the actual branch, assuming the same command decisions.

### Breakout
A stronger outside/inside tank force and better heavy transport give:
- temporary corridor duration around **4–6 hours**, rather than 2–4;
- another **700–1,200 personnel** plausibly extracted beyond the actual branch result;
- more guns, tractors and wounded evacuated.

### Campaign result
**SOVIET OPERATIONAL VICTORY STILL MOST LIKELY.**

Planning-band outcome:
- Japanese combat casualties: roughly **13.8–14.7k**;
- Soviet/Mongolian combat casualties: roughly **27.1–27.8k**;
- personnel casualty exchange: approximately **1.84–2.01:1** in Japan's favor;
- Japanese dead/missing: roughly **5.8–6.5k**;
- Japanese tanks physically surviving/recoverable east of the battlefield: roughly **44–52**.

The disputed battlefield is still lost.

## 7. Staff conclusion

The study rejects the proposition:
"Had we merely possessed enough Chi-Ha and turbochargers, we would have won Nomonhan."

It accepts:
"Enough Chi-Ha and turbo-diesel heavy vehicles would have made the force substantially harder to destroy and materially improved the casualty exchange and breakout."

The limiting factors after those two improvements become even clearer:
1. long-range artillery;
2. artillery ammunition volume;
3. motor-transport quantity, not just quality;
4. field radio density and combined-arms command;
5. high-velocity anti-tank/tank gun;
6. bridge/road/water logistics.

The most important technical lesson is that turbocharging the **support fleet** gives more campaign-level return than turbocharging the tank merely to gain additional horsepower.
For the tank itself, the next decisive requirement is not more engine power but a high-velocity anti-armor gun plus communications and combined-arms control.
