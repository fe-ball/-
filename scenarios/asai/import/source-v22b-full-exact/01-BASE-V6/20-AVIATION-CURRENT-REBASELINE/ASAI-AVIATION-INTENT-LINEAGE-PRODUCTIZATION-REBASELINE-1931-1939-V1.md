# 0. Central correction

Asai aviation is not a sequence of unrelated aircraft.

It is a set of **problem-solving lineages**.

The same five questions recur:

1. **fuel / range** — how to turn turbine fuel flexibility and efficiency into usable radius;
2. **landing / field length** — what happens when fuel, payload and speed increase landing energy;
3. **propulsion form** — pure jet, aft-fan and turboprop are different allocations of the same core capability;
4. **structure/material** — how to move new material from coupons to real production without losing attribution;
5. **productization** — when to freeze a useful lower technology block even though the research frontier is already moving.

Aircraft exist because one of these questions needs a flying answer or a customer product.

Do not generate an aircraft simply because a new E-generation exists.

Do not delay a useful aircraft simply because the next E-generation is already visible.

# 1. Asai's aviation vision

## 1.1 High end and broad base are deliberately different

Asai aviation has two simultaneous commercial/technical goals.

### High end — "尖り"
Purpose:
- speed;
- high altitude;
- interception;
- strategic reconnaissance;
- propulsion/system proof.

Typical vehicles:
- E4/E5/E6 twin line;
- E5/E6 single high-speed line;
- pure jet / aft-fan comparisons.

These aircraft tolerate:
- higher cost;
- low numbers;
- heavy instrumentation;
- rapid block changes.

### Broad base — "広がり"
Purpose:
- replace aviation gasoline with kerosene in ordinary flying;
- build turbine-maintenance literacy;
- accumulate customer operating hours;
- solve rough-field/deck/landing problems;
- sell enough aircraft that manuals, spares and training become real.

Typical vehicle:
- small turboprop practical / short-field family.

This aircraft deliberately does **not** chase the research frontier.

It is the aviation equivalent of Asai's successful industrial product strategy:
**sell the mature answer while the laboratory develops the next one.**

# 2. Fuel first — performance dividends are not automatically converted to speed

## 2.1 Default allocation rule

For Asai-supported military aircraft, an improvement in:
- engine efficiency;
- structural mass;
- propulsive efficiency;
- internal volume;
- drag;

is not automatically booked as maximum speed.

The first allocation question is:

> Does this mission need more speed, or does it need more radius, reserve, payload, protection, or reliability?

For Japan, range and fuel security have unusually high strategic value.

Therefore an important recurring Asai choice is:
**use part of the performance dividend as fuel.**

This is already natural for turbine aircraft because:
- kerosene is denser than aviation gasoline;
- nonvolatile fuel makes external/drop tanks less hazardous;
- internal fuel placement can be less constrained by gasoline-vapor fire risk;
- turboprop/aft-fan efficiency can convert the same tankage into greater mission radius.

## 2.2 Fuel is a mission-setting knob

Three standard configurations should exist rather than one "maximum range" aircraft:

### Clean / light
- less fuel;
- best climb/acceleration;
- point-defense/interception.

### Internal mission fuel
- ordinary operational radius;
- reserve/alternate.

### Ferry / extended
- jettisonable external kerosene tanks or mission-specific extra fuel;
- tanks discarded before combat/landing when necessary.

This prevents "long range" from being permanently welded into every sortie.

# 3. Fuel creates the landing problem

Increasing fuel is not free.

For fixed wing area and CLmax:

**Vstall ∝ sqrt(W / (S·CLmax))**

Therefore:
- +10% landing weight → roughly +4.9% stall/approach-speed scale;
- +20% landing weight → roughly +9.5%.

The aircraft kinetic energy that landing gear, brakes or an arresting system must absorb is:

**E = 1/2 · m · V²**

Since V² itself rises approximately with W at the same CLmax:
- +10% landing weight can mean roughly **+21% arresting/impact energy**;
- +20% can mean roughly **+44%**.

Thus a design habit of "use the gain as more fuel" naturally creates a second problem:

**how do we safely return a heavy aircraft?**

This is the missing causal link between the fuel/range program and the short-field/STOL line.

---

# 4. Landing solutions are layered

Asai does not make every aircraft a STOL aircraft.

It uses the cheapest solution compatible with mission.

## Layer 1 — operational fuel management
- jettisonable external tanks;
- internal fuel-dump capability where a large/high-value prototype needs rejected-takeoff/early-return protection;
- mission fuel rules rather than always filling every tank.

## Layer 2 — ordinary aircraft engineering
- larger flaps/slats where justified;
- stronger brakes;
- robust undercarriage;
- wider gear track;
- low-speed control authority;
- visibility;
- approach procedure and instrumentation.

## Layer 3 — dedicated low-speed aircraft
When the **mission itself** demands:
- rough/short strips;
- carrier or short deck;
- frequent training landings;
- liaison/observation near the front;

then high-lift/short-field capability becomes the primary design objective.

This is the origin of the Asai STOL practical family.

STOL is therefore not an isolated wonder-aircraft concept.

It is Asai's **landing-technology school**.

# 5. Small turboprop product lineage — from practical sale aircraft to STOL

This line must be separated from the Material-series test aircraft.

## Block P0 — propulsion/practical mules, 1933–35

After E4 turboprop bench work, conventional aircraft are used to learn:
- free-power-turbine behavior;
- reduction gear;
- propeller governor;
- fuel/control;
- restart;
- vibration;
- maintenance.

Not customer products.

## Block P1 — small practical turboprop, 1935–37

Purpose:
**make an aircraft ordinary people outside Asai can actually operate.**

Characteristics:
- modest speed;
- fixed/simple landing gear;
- conservative structure;
- partial plant-fiber/epoxy secondary parts where already safe;
- kerosene;
- simple maintenance access;
- good low-speed behavior but not yet the full later STOL high-lift package.

This is the branch that current history correctly preserves as:
**external evaluation / small-sale territory by summer 1937.**

It is a real product-development aircraft, not merely a testbed.

## Block P2 — short-field practical / STOL production block, 1937–39

The P1 customer and military experience is combined with the landing-energy problem.

New design priorities:
- lower wing loading;
- Fowler/slat-type high lift;
- robust rough-field gear;
- braking and low-speed directional control;
- high static propeller thrust;
- simple/mechanically passive powered-flow assistance only where safe;
- no dependency on a complex powered-lift mechanism for survival.

The old archived exact STOL specification is **not automatically restored as numerical canon**.

What is restored is the design logic:
**speed is deliberately traded for landing/field/deck utility because speed is not the limiting axis for liaison, observation, training and utility work.**

## Relationship between P1 and P2

They are:
- the **same commercial lineage**;
- not necessarily the identical airframe.

P1 is the first sellable turbine practical aircraft.
P2 is the purpose-designed short-field evolution.

This resolves the old "small TP sale aircraft = Material No.1 = STOL?" confusion.

They are not the same object.

# 7. Why E5 can be produced while E6 exists

Asai separates:

### Research frontier
moves continuously:
E5 → E6 → E7 component work.

### Product shelf
freezes deliberately for a defined interval:
- known engine rating;
- known propeller;
- stable controls;
- stable maintenance procedure;

Therefore **E5 remains the correct production engine even after E6 flies.**

This is intentional product engineering, not technological backwardness.

# 9. Aft-fan — the previous timing was too slow

## 9.1 Terminology

The aft-fan is itself a turbofan/bypass engine in physical principle.

What Asai deliberately avoids in the early period is the **front-fan / multi-spool conventional architecture** that requires:
- front fan integration;
- through-shaft/coaxial layout;
- additional compressor-spool matching or gearing;
- more difficult inlet/front-frame structure.

The chosen shortcut is:

**single-spool gas generator
→ separate free LP turbine
→ direct-drive aft fan**

This still has an extra rotating spool.
It is not "no mechanism."

But it reuses the free-power-turbine knowledge already developed for turboprops and shaft products.

## 9.2 Correct causal timing

Because Asai already has:
- E4 turboprop/free-power-turbine work in 1931–33;
- E4 twin jet/system flight work in 1932–33;
- a known desire to improve jet static thrust and fuel economy;

aft-fan research should begin much earlier than the previous Gate 68/69/76 sequence.

### Corrected timeline

**1933–34**
- conceptual and cold-flow/fan rig work;
- E4-scale free-turbine/fan experiments;
- no need to make an aircraft yet.

**1934–35**
- E4/E5 transition full-scale aft-fan ground rig;
- fan/stator geometry;
- free-turbine matching;
- overspeed/control;
- rear-duct losses.

At E4 scale, flight utility may still be poor after mass/drag, so ground research is sufficient.

**1935–36**
- E5 full-scale aft-fan rig becomes a serious propulsion candidate;
- military E5 twin requirement includes provision for pure-jet versus aft-fan comparison;
- flight nacelle/interface work proceeds in parallel with No.1.

**1936**
- second development airframe should be planned/authorized as part of the military twin program, not postponed until end-1937.

**1937 Q1–Q2**
- E5 twin No.1 pure-jet first flight / baseline.

**1937 Q4**
- E5 twin No.2 aft-fan first-flight working band.

This replaces the previous "no aft-fan flight in 1937 / first flight Sep–Oct 1938" closure.

## 9.3 What 1938 should mean

By 1938 the question is no longer:
"does aft-fan work at all?"

The work is:
- durability;
- fan blade/stator life;
- spool matching;
- thermal environment;
- starting/restart;
- control transients;
- mission fuel;
- maintenance;
- pure-jet vs aft-fan sortie comparison.

Thus by 1938 H1 the aft-fan should already be a flying development package.

By 1938 H2 it can be mature enough to inform E6 design directly.

# 11. Pure jet versus aft-fan — the vision

They are not sequential replacements.

Asai intentionally keeps both.

## Pure jet
Use when:
- maximum high-speed performance matters;
- mission is short;
- low-altitude/static thrust is less important;
- fuel can be spent.

## Aft-fan
Use when:
- takeoff/climb/static thrust matter;
- mission radius matters;
- repeated sorties matter;
- a modest speed penalty is acceptable.

This is why No.1 pure jet remains valuable even after No.2 aft-fan succeeds.

It is a controlled baseline, not an obsolete aircraft.

# 12. Material lineage — separate structure development from product aircraft

## M0 — resin/process foundation, 1931–34
- epoxy synthesis/adhesion;
- paper/honeycomb processes;
- plant-fiber sorting;
- coupons;
- bonded secondary structures.

## M1 — product infiltration, 1934–37
Selected practical aircraft receive:
- fairings;
- ducts;
- covers;
- floor/partition;
- tanks;
- secondary skin/panels;
- control-surface secondary parts.

Primary load paths remain:
- wood;
- steel;
- aluminum.

This is how the P1 small TP can be a partially plant-FRP aircraft by 1937 without being an all-composite airplane.

## Material No.1 — low-speed structural A/B aircraft, 1938

Purpose:
**same aerodynamic/propulsion baseline, new structure.**

Use the P2/STOL aerodynamic/engine family so that:
- low-speed loads;
- rough-field cycles;
- moisture;
- landing shocks;
- maintenance damage;

can be compared directly against a conventional structure.

It is **not the sale aircraft**.

It is a test article derived from the sale/STOL platform.

Correct chronology remains approximately:
- design authorization end-1937;
- ~50–70% build by 1938-06-30;
- completion/first flight in 1938 H2.

By 1939 it should be accumulating aging/repair/field-cycle data.

## Material No.2 — high dynamic pressure structural aircraft, 1938–39

Purpose:
- same material system under much higher dynamic pressure;
- vibration/flutter;
- jet heat boundary;
- bonded joints;
- stiffness, not top speed.

It follows Material No.1 because low-speed proof does not validate high-q structure.

Working chronology:
- design begins 1938 H2;
- construction/testing during 1939;
- first-flight gate 1939 H2 / early-1940 depending structural proof.

Do not make it a service fighter.

## What production aircraft inherit

Production aircraft receive only **validated material blocks**:
- secondary panels;
- floors;
- doors;
- ducts;
- tank structures;
- selected sandwich panels.

They do not wait for the whole Material-series program to finish.

This is the material equivalent of product-block freezing.

# 13. Material evolution after 1939

The next material steps are not "Material No.3 = all-composite fighter."

The natural progression is:

1. plant-fiber/epoxy secondary structures;
2. paper-honeycomb sandwich;
3. bonded metal/wood and metal-face sandwich;
4. limited glass-reinforced high-value areas after coupling/qualification;
5. only after years of fatigue/moisture/repair data: larger load-bearing structural use.

Carbon/graphitized fiber remains:
- local stiffener;
- test coupon;
- high-value small parts;

not a 1939 mass airframe material.

# 14. Productionization doctrine

This is the missing answer to "if technology evolves this fast, when can anything be mass-produced?"

Asai uses **four gates**, not "wait until technology stops improving."

## Gate A — mission value
Does the aircraft solve a real customer problem **now**?

Examples:
- P2 STOL: yes — rough strips/decks/training/liaison.
- E5 twin: yes as military technical evaluation, but broad role may still be unsettled.
- E6 twin: potentially yes for a narrowed military role after tests.

## Gate B — bounded failure modes
Not "perfect reliability."

The remaining failures must be:
- known;
- detectable;
- maintainable;
- non-catastrophic at acceptable frequency;
- supportable by spares/training.

## Gate C — configuration stability
The basic:
- airframe geometry;
- engine interface;
- controls;
- landing gear;
- fuel system;

must stop moving enough that jigs/manuals/spares are not obsolete immediately.

The research frontier may still be advancing.

## Gate D — ecosystem
Can the customer actually operate it?
- fuel;
- trained maintainers;
- spare engines/modules;
- tools;
- manuals;
- fields/decks;
- radios/mission equipment.

A technically superior aircraft that fails Gate D does not deserve mass production.

# 15. Production levels

## Level 0 — research aircraft
Purpose: answer a physical question.
No production intent.

Examples:
- E5 future-form single;
- Material No.1/No.2.

## Level 1 — military technical prototypes / development aircraft
Customer requirement exists, but final service configuration is not fixed.

Example:
- 1937 E5 twin program.

## Level 2 — pilot / service-evaluation batch
Typically several to low-tens.

Purpose:
- train customer personnel;
- learn field maintenance;
- validate real mission cycle;
- establish parts/manuals.

This stage must be used more often in the worldline.
It prevents the false binary:
"prototype forever" versus "mass production immediately."

## Level 3 — limited series product
Tens/year or low hundreds/year depending class.

A mature but not frontier technology is ideal here.

Example:
- P2 small TP/STOL family by 1939.

## Level 4 — mass series
Only when:
- mission is institutionally stable;
- support system exists;
- configuration has a useful production lifetime.

The latest engine generation is not required.

# 20. The causal map in one page

### Propulsion problem
Pure jet is fast but short-ranged and weak at low speed.

↓  
Asai first uses:
- more/denser kerosene;
- external fuel;
- mission-specific fuel loading.

↓  
Range improves, but:
- takeoff weight rises;
- aborted-mission landing weight rises;
- landing/arresting energy rises.

↓  
Two responses split:

**High-speed line**
- fuel jettison;
- stronger gear/brakes;
- acceptable higher approach speed where runway permits;
- aft-fan for better low-speed thrust/TSFC.

**Utility/carrier/rough-field line**
- low wing loading;
- high lift;
- robust gear;
- STOL.

Meanwhile:

**Propulsion efficiency response**
- front-fan/multi-spool architecture is deferred;
- existing free-power-turbine knowledge is reused;
- aft-fan starts early and flies by 1937.

And:

**Material response**
- plant-FRP first enters ordinary secondary parts;
- Material No.1 proves larger low-speed sandwich structures;
- Material No.2 proves high-q behavior;
- only proven blocks migrate into customer aircraft.

This is one integrated program, not a set of unrelated aircraft rewards.
