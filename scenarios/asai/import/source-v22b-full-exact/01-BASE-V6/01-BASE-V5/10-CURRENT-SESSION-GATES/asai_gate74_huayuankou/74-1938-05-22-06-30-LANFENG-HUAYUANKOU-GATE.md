# Gate 74 — 1938-05-22 to 1938-06-30
## 蘭封・開封・花園口決壊 / 黄河による作戦軸転換

Status: CLOSED at calendar/site/strategic-effect level.

## 1. Lanfeng: Asai helps the isolated 14th Division, but does not create a new outcome

The Japanese 14th Division had already crossed the Yellow River during the Xuzhou operation and advanced deeply into eastern Honan.
Chinese forces then concentrated roughly a dozen divisions around the Lanfeng sector in an attempt to destroy it.

In the Asai worldline the 14th Division enters this episode with:
- somewhat healthier towing and vehicle readiness,
- slightly better artillery availability,
- lower maintenance debt,
- a small preserved veteran/specialist margin.

This matters because the division is isolated.
Its ammunition and repair loop can continue slightly longer than matched history.

But China historically failed to destroy it anyway.
Therefore Asai cannot be credited with “saving” a division that already escaped destruction.

Ruling:
- Lanfeng seizure/recapture/counterattack sequence remains in the same May 21–31 band.
- Chinese assaults cost roughly **200–500 extra casualties/disables** relative to matched history.
- Japanese 14th-Division-side losses are roughly **250–450 lower**.
- The operational result is unchanged: the Chinese encirclement attempt fails and the Chinese mass withdraws west around the end of May / 1 June.

## 2. Victory/failure psychology now cuts both ways

Chinese command has three recent lessons:
1. Shanghai: withdraw before full closure and preserve the army.
2. Taierzhuang: a conspicuous stand can produce a real political and military victory.
3. Lanfeng: mass concentration does not guarantee annihilation of an isolated Japanese division if command cohesion is poor.

Thus by the end of May the Chinese leadership is not simply “more willing to retreat” or “more willing to stand.”
It is more sensitive to the geometry of encirclement and more desperate about preserving Wuhan/Zhengzhou.

This helps explain why an extreme geographic solution becomes acceptable without changing its historical date.

## 3. The dike decision is not attrition-limited

The Huayuankou decision should not be modeled as:
“Japanese trucks are a few percent better → China immediately blows the river earlier.”

The decision requires:
- strategic approval,
- secrecy,
- engineer/labor mobilization,
- selection of a breach point,
- explosives/tools,
- and a river/dike that will actually open.

The Japanese threat is marginally sharper in the Asai worldline, but the historical threat was already existential.

Therefore the high-level decision stays around **1 June**.

## 4. Zhaokou failures survive intact

Historical flood scholarship based on Nationalist records places two failed dike-opening attempts at Zhaokou on **4–6 June**.

They fail because of physical conditions:
- dike construction,
- revetment/foundation,
- excavation/blasting difficulty,
- low or shifting water level/current,
- local sand/bar conditions.

Asai is on the Japanese side and does not improve Chinese hydraulic engineering.

Even if Japanese spearheads are several hours more threatening, those failures still occur.

This is a hard anti-butterfly gate:
**no “earlier Asai advance → successful first blast at Zhaokou.”**

## 5. Kaifeng

Foreign diplomatic reporting records Japanese occupation of Kaifeng around noon on **6 June**.

Asai local readiness may move combat/clearing by hours, but not enough for a 5 June capture.

Kaifeng remains:
**CLOSED-SAME-DATE: 6 June.**

## 6. Huayuankou

After the Zhaokou failures, work shifts to Huayuankou north of Zhengzhou.

Water begins spilling through the breach on **9 June**.

Worldline sensitivity:
- urgency and continuous labor might make the final opening perhaps **0–8 hours earlier**;
- do not lock an exact hour;
- calendar day remains 9 June.

The river initially moves slowly and then expands with rising water and summer rains.

An hours-scale difference at the breach is not enough to justify a different long-term flood course.

## 7. Civilian catastrophe: do not turn hours into hundreds of thousands of counterfactual deaths

This gate explicitly rejects a false precision.

Historical postwar studies cited by Micah Muscolino estimate:
- nearly four million displaced,
- more than 800,000 deaths across the wartime flood/famine/disease disaster,
- about 35% of cultivated land inundated across 44 affected counties in the postwar tabulation.

Those deaths are not “890,000 people who drowned on 9 June.”
They accumulate through drowning, disease, hunger and the nine-year hydraulic disaster.

Because the Asai worldline keeps:
- the same breach site,
- the same 9 June date,
- the same minimal-warning policy,
- essentially the same hydrological route,

the civilian/ecological delta is treated as **first-order zero**.
A sub-percent difference from hours of timing is OPEN and is not quantified.

This is much safer than inventing tens of thousands of additional or saved civilians.

## 8. What China actually buys with the flood

The price is not mainly a propaganda asset.

The Nationalist regime initially conceals responsibility and publicly blames Japanese action.
Immediate political-accounting therefore does not capture the real cost.

The real cost is:
- millions of refugees,
- destruction of agricultural production,
- loss of tax/resource base,
- epidemic/famine burden,
- hydraulic-control burden through 1947,
- military and civilian labor diverted to dikes/relief,
- long-term damage to Henan/Anhui/Jiangsu state capacity.

In return, China buys **time and geography**.

By 15 June the Japanese spokesman himself acknowledges that flooding has completely checked the advance toward Zhengzhou.

That means Asai's motorization advantage is almost perfectly neutralized at the route level:
a truck that is 5% more reliable still cannot drive through a newly created flood sea.

## 9. Japanese strategic reorientation

The flood prevents the intended direct northeast-to-Zhengzhou/Pinghan route from serving as the main road toward Wuhan.

By late June foreign reporting notes:
- the Chengzhou thrust has been abandoned,
- some forces are moving east/south toward the Nanjing/Yangtze axis,
- others are reoriented northward.

This remains the same in the worldline.

Asai changes the readiness with which units reorient, not the fundamental choice of route.

## 10. Numerical state at 30 June

Japanese:
- cumulative battle-casualty avoidance rises from ~4,800 to **~5,300 center**;
- current China-theater headcount advantage ~**+1,300** after replacement absorption.

Chinese military:
- prior manpower delta was already near zero after Xuzhou;
- slightly more expensive Lanfeng fighting moves the center to roughly **-100 effective personnel vs matched history**, with a very wide band;
- prior preserved experienced-cadre advantage remains mildly positive at ~**+240 center**.

Civilian flood losses are deliberately held outside the military manpower ledger.

## End state

Clock: **1938-06-30 late evening.**

The direct Japanese drive west toward Zhengzhou has been broken by the Yellow River flood.
The China war's operational center of gravity shifts toward the Yangtze/Wuhan campaign.

Asai has reached a revealing limit:
its industrial advantage can improve machines, repair, artillery availability and unit endurance,
but it cannot preserve an operational route after the defender converts an entire river basin into a barrier.

Next major gate:
**Wuhan campaign buildup and the Yangtze advance, July 1938 onward.**
