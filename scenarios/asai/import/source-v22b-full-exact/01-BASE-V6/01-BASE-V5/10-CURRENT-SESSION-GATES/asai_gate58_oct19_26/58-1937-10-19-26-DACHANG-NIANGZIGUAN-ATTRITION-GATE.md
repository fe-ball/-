# Gate 58 — 1937-10-19 to 1937-10-26
## Shanghai: Guangxi counteroffensive → Dachang fall
## North China: Niangzi Pass breakthrough

Status: WORKING-CLOSED at operational/calendar level; detailed casualty figures remain OPEN.

### Core ruling
- Shanghai: The Chinese counteroffensive of 21–23 October still occurs. Newly arrived Guangxi formations suffer rapid cohesion loss; division names survive, but multiple regimental/battalion groupings fall to D/reconstitution status.
- Dachang: falls on 26 October as historically. Asai effects may move local fire-support/position-collapse timings by roughly 1–4 hours, but do not shift the date.
- Chapei/Kiangwan withdrawal: begins during the night of 26 October after Dachang is lost; date unchanged.
- Niangzi Pass: falls on 26 October. Asai improves the 20th Division's artillery/transport sustainment, but mountain terrain and infantry fighting dominate. Calendar date unchanged; local timing may advance by ~1–3 hours.
- 109th Division: pack-horse shortage remains a hard bottleneck. Asai motor-vehicle advantages do not substitute for pack transport on mountain tracks; no early deployment is granted.

### Attrition-model interpretation
P = personnel / junior-leader quality / fatigue
F = fire-support availability
M = mobility / transport readiness
S = supply / maintenance depth

Asai primarily raises F/M/S by a few points. P changes only indirectly through somewhat lower casualties and less mechanical-exhaustion burden.

### Shanghai readiness by 26 October (working)
Japanese veteran formations remain badly worn:
- 3rd/11th Divisions: C+ to B- boundary; Asai does not restore them to fresh offensive status.
- 101st Division: C-range; continued use is possible, but it is not a fresh exploitation formation.
- 9th Division: B- range.
- 13th Division: B range, comparatively fresher.

Chinese:
- old Central Army divisions around Dachang: C/C- overall, with D-level battalions and consolidated remnants common.
- Guangxi 21st Group Army: enters the counteroffensive around A-/B+ in freshness, then drops sharply; by 24–26 October many component regiments/battalions require consolidation. Whole corps/divisions are not declared annihilated.
- 18th Division and other Dachang defenders: organizationally survive the battle, but are heavily degraded by the fall of the position.

### Niangzi Pass readiness by 26 October (working)
- Japanese 20th Division: B-/B. Human fatigue remains significant, but artillery, selected transport, repair and supply are somewhat healthier in the Asai worldline.
- Chinese Niangzi Pass defense: operational cohesion breaks on 26 October; some regimental/battalion groupings fall to D/reconstitution status. Japanese claims of destroying 'about two divisions' are treated as an operational-collapse claim, not literal annihilation.
- Japanese 109th Division: mobility constrained by horse losses; this remains essentially unchanged.

### Casualty-delta handling
Do not apply a global multiplier.
- Shanghai 19–26 Oct: Japanese battle casualties plausibly ~4–7% below the matched baseline in sectors where naval/army fire support and equipment readiness are relevant.
- Chinese casualties are not globally increased. In the Guangxi counteroffensive and final Dachang fighting, a small additional loss/cohesion penalty can push already stressed subunits across C→D thresholds.
- Niangzi Pass: Japanese losses may be ~2–5% lower in relevant supported actions, but terrain and close infantry combat cap the benefit.

### End-state
Clock: 1937-10-26 late evening.
- Dachang lost; Chinese withdrawal from Chapei/Kiangwan is commencing.
- Niangzi Pass lost; the eastern Shanxi defensive system is withdrawing and the Japanese 20th Division is preparing pursuit toward Pingding/Yangquan.
- Asai has not created a new division or a one-day calendar lead. The meaningful divergence is that several Japanese formations retain slightly more usable F/M/S while some Chinese subunits cross reconstitution thresholds marginally earlier.
