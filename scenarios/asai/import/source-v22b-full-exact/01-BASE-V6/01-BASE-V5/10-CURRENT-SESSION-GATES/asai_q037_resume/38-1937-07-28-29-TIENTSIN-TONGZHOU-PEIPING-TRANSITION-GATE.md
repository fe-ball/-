## Historical baseline — Peiping
Japanese operational reconstruction states that Chinese forces around Peiping began withdrawing south on the evening of 28 July and had essentially left the left bank of the Yungting River by 29 July. The Sakai Brigade continued pursuit on 29 July while Japanese forces were redistributed around Peiping, Fengtai, and Tungchow.

The Japanese plan had explicitly avoided a major assault on the walled city because of the defensive value of the walls, danger to Japanese residents, and the desire to preserve order. Contemporary U.S. diplomatic reporting on 29 July confirms the sudden political transition: Song Zheyuan had departed, while Zhang Zizhong remained in charge locally.

### Gate decision
Peiping changes hands through **withdrawal, occupation pressure, and political transition**, not a major alternate siege. Asai technology does not change this geometry.

## Historical baseline — Tientsin night attack
The Chinese 38th Division elements south of Tientsin, together with Tientsin security forces, attacked Japanese positions during the night of 28/29 July. Japanese operational records list the Japanese concession, China Garrison Army headquarters, the airfield, and other installations among the targets.

There is a source-timing conflict that must be preserved:
- the postwar Japanese monograph compresses the sequence and says the attackers were counterattacked by reserve forces and bombed at dawn on 29 July and then withdrew;
- contemporary U.S. Consul General Caldwell reported that firing became serious around 02:00, that Japanese aircraft heavily bombed targets through 29 July, and at 19:00 still judged the Japanese concession in grave danger and expected serious fighting that night.

Therefore this branch does **not** close the Battle of Tientsin at dawn on the 29th. The correct gate interpretation is:
1. Chinese night attack achieves local surprise and temporary penetrations/pressure;
2. Japanese reserve and air counteraction begin stabilizing key installations around dawn/daylight;
3. urban fighting and bombing continue through the day;
4. final Chinese withdrawal/fall of Tientsin belongs to the next gate (29 night–30 Jul).

## Asai delta — Tientsin

### 1. Airfield resilience — real but bounded
The Japanese Monograph notes that the main strength of the Provisional Air Corps formed in Japan reached Tientsin via Manchuria on 27 July. Thus, by the night of 28/29 July, Tientsin is a place where the previously closed Asai-worldline aviation-serviceability delta can actually matter.

Use only the previously established delta:
- engine/QC/maintenance practice raises serviceability by a few percentage points;
- no jet or gas-turbine combat aircraft;
- no automatic change in aircraft type, speed, bombload, or pilot skill.

Operational consequence under a night airfield attack:
- a few more aircraft are capable of taxiing/dispersing/taking off once crews are alerted;
- dawn counter-sortie generation recovers slightly faster;
- ground aircraft losses are directionally somewhat lower than a historical-equivalent execution.

**Do not close an absolute aircraft-loss number.** Chinese accounts sometimes claim destruction of more than ten Japanese aircraft, while the consulted Japanese operational source does not provide a matching loss total. Working sensitivity only: Asai readiness could save or return to service **roughly 1–3 aircraft-equivalents**, but this is not canon.

### 2. Reserve motor transport / recovery
Some Asai-turbo heavy trucks, tractors, workshop or recovery vehicles may already be present in North China, but exact Tientsin allocation remains OPEN.

Permitted effect:
- slightly fewer noncombat breakdowns during reserve assembly and ammunition/recovery movement;
- local reserve elements can sustain their counterattack with marginally less mechanical friction.

Not permitted:
- a large earlier counterattack time without unit-level transport evidence;
- turning all Tientsin vehicles into Asai-equipped vehicles.

### 3. Emergency power and communications — do not overclaim
Current branch has only **5–12 small mobile GT power sets/year** as a limited-delivery working quantity, and no CLOSED allocation placing one specifically at Tientsin airfield or China Garrison Army headquarters.

Therefore Q-037 does **not** assume Tientsin has a magic Asai emergency-power network. Physical wire cuts, local fighting, and command disruption remain real. If a later site-allocation audit places an Asai set at Tientsin, the effect can be revisited, but it is not needed for this gate.

### 4. Net tactical effect
Japanese positions still suffer surprise and local damage. The Asai delta appears mainly **after** the initial shock:
- slightly more usable aircraft at first light;
- slightly cleaner maintenance and re-arming recovery;
- marginally more reliable support vehicles;
- somewhat faster restoration of local operating tempo.

This is enough for **MINOR-RESILIENCE-DELTA**, not enough to erase the Chinese attack or end the city battle hours earlier.

## Tongzhou incident — event preservation
On 29 July, elements of the East Hopei Peace Preservation Corps at Tongzhou mutiny and attack Japanese military/special-service positions and Japanese/Korean residents. Historical accounts differ over detailed causation and casualty totals. Factors cited include accumulated anti-Japanese hostility, contacts or expectations involving the 29th Army, the 27 July fighting and mistaken Japanese bombing of East Hopei troops, misinformation about Japanese defeat, and the fact that the main Japanese force had been drawn away from Tongzhou.

The branch therefore does **not** assign one single trigger as uniquely causal.

### Why Asai does not prevent it
The decisive conditions are not industrial:
- the local Japanese force is thin after major forces move toward the Peiping/Nanyuan fighting;
- the mutiny is an internal political/military betrayal by forces previously treated as friendly;
- the attack begins before dawn with surprise;
- communications are physically cut or disrupted;
- relief is delayed by the wider Peiping–Tientsin battle.

There is no CLOSED setting that the Tongzhou garrison has an Asai GT generator, an Asai radio network, or a materially larger garrison. Better Japanese industrial QC cannot make a nominally friendly security force cease to mutiny.

### Relief timing and casualty effect
Historical accounts place the mutiny attack in the early hours of 29 July and Japanese air action only later in the day, with effective land relief extending into 30 July. The response lag is therefore dominated by **detection, communications, distance, and competing combat commitments**, not aircraft engine reliability.

Asai aviation readiness can marginally improve the number of aircraft available once the relief mission is actually ordered, but it arrives too late to prevent the main civilian killings.

Canon decision:
- Tongzhou incident still occurs on **29 July**;
- Japanese/Korean civilian and military losses remain in the **same broad historical order of magnitude (200+ total deaths in the commonly cited range)**;
- exact total and breakdown remain OPEN-detail;
- no defensible basis exists for a large Asai-driven casualty reduction.

## Political effect
Because the Tongzhou event still occurs and civilian losses remain of the same order, its Japanese domestic political and press effect remains materially intact: outrage hardens public attitudes and further narrows the political space for a localized settlement.

Q-037 does not yet quantify a separate cabinet/Army decision change from Tongzhou; it closes only the **direction** of the political effect. Any later escalation decision must still be evaluated at its own gate.
