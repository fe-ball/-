# Gate 61 — 1937-11-06 to 1937-11-09
## Shanghai withdrawal decision / Sungkiang crisis
## Taiyuan assault and fall

Status: CLOSED at date/strategic level. Withdrawal-order hour and detailed retreat losses remain WORKING.

### Method correction: retreat is not modeled as attrition-limited
The Shanghai withdrawal trigger is decomposed into:
1. command/political decision,
2. perceived encirclement risk from Hangchow Bay,
3. route capacity and traffic control,
4. communications/order dissemination,
5. unit cohesion and rear-guard ability.

Attrition affects (4)-(5) and the cost/orderliness of withdrawal, but does not by itself determine the date of the withdrawal order.

### Shanghai ruling
Historical anchors:
- By 8 Nov evening, Japanese forces from Hangchow Bay had advanced rapidly, crossed the Whangpoo southeast of Sungkiang, and Chinese reserves were concentrating around Sungkiang.
- Chinese central command issues the general withdrawal order on 8 Nov.
- The main forces south/west of Soochow Creek begin an orderly withdrawal around midnight 8/9 Nov.
- By 9 Nov Japanese forces occupy much of the immediate Shanghai area; the withdrawal is attributed by foreign observers to the rapid Hangchow Bay thrust toward Sungkiang.

Asai-worldline:
- 6th Division enters the operation with somewhat better F/M/S after its North China service and transfer.
- 10th Army unloading-to-march readiness and selected artillery/vehicle availability are modestly higher.
- This may move the perceived encirclement threshold forward by roughly 3–8 hours.
- Political/diplomatic considerations and command inertia still prevent a 7 Nov general retreat order.
- Working ruling: the 8 Nov withdrawal order can plausibly be issued earlier within the day; some formations start disengaging in the evening rather than near midnight.
- The general calendar remains 8 Nov order / night 8–9 Nov withdrawal.
- Earlier execution can modestly reduce losses among formations that still retain route discipline, partly offsetting the Japanese tactical attrition advantage.
- Severely degraded C-/D formations remain prone to route congestion, loss of contact and abandonment of heavy equipment.

### Sungkiang
- The Sungkiang crisis remains acute on 8–10 Nov.
- 67th Army / attached defenders serve as a delaying force and suffer catastrophic cohesion loss.
- Asai does not magically destroy them earlier; the Japanese 6th Division's better readiness mainly helps bypass/press northward while leaving forces to contain Sungkiang.
- No clean one-day shift in Sungkiang fall is granted at this gate.

### Taiyuan ruling
Historical anchors:
- 6 Nov: Japanese 5th Division reaches Taiyuan/Yangchu gates and prepares assault.
- 8 Nov: general assault; Japanese troops occupy city walls shortly after 0900 and fight inside.
- 9 Nov: Japanese occupation of Taiyuan is reported complete.

Asai-worldline:
- 5th Division P is low from Xinkou; Asai cannot restore infantry freshness.
- F/S are modestly healthier, so artillery preparation and sustained support can move some assault phases ahead by ~1–2 hours.
- City-wall assault, close combat and Chinese breakout decision dominate final timing.
- Taiyuan: same 8 Nov breach / 9 Nov complete occupation calendar.

### Attrition/readiness consequences
Shanghai Chinese retreat:
- Old Central Army formations: many C/D subunits; retreat order remains command-driven.
- Earlier within-day disengagement may save a small number of personnel and reduce some encirclement losses.
- Equipment losses remain high because heavy weapons/vehicles are constrained by route congestion and exhausted transport.
- Do not apply a blanket increase to Chinese casualties for Asai effects during the retreat.

Japanese:
- 6th Division: A-/B+ relative state, with Asai advantage concentrated in F/M/S.
- Shanghai veteran divisions: remain C+/B-; they do not become fresh pursuit formations.
- 5th Division at Taiyuan: B-/C+ in P, stronger F/S; no date shift.

### End-state
Clock: 1937-11-09 late evening.
- Shanghai: general Chinese withdrawal from the immediate metropolitan front is under way / largely completed in the city approaches; Japanese forces occupy broad areas south of Soochow Creek. The main Chinese problem is now preserving formations while falling back toward Kunshan/Wufu-line directions.
- Sungkiang: fighting/containment continues in the wider sector; delaying forces are severely degraded.
- Taiyuan: complete Japanese occupation is achieved on 9 Nov; surviving Chinese forces withdraw west/south.
- First meaningful Asai effect on retreat timing: an intra-day, not calendar-day, shift; importantly, this may LOWER some Chinese retreat losses by enabling an earlier disengagement once the political decision is made.
