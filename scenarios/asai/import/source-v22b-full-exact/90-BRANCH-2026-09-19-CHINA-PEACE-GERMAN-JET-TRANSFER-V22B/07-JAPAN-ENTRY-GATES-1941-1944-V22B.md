# V22B — JAPAN ANTI-SOVIET ENTRY GATES, 1941–1944

## 1. Why entry probability rises

Japan is no longer a distant political ally. It is a technology/industrial stakeholder in German survival.

By 1943:

- German airpower visibly benefits from Japanese technology transfer;
- German return technology is valuable to Japan;
- sunk German survival would destroy an important technical and strategic partnership;
- Japan has ended the China war and is not consumed by a Pacific war;
- the southern resource system remains commercial rather than conquered.

This makes pro-entry arguments much stronger than in the original branch.

## 2. Why non-entry remains rational

Japanese armed neutrality already yields major benefits:

- pins large Soviet Far East forces;
- costs Japan almost no combat casualties;
- preserves trade with US/UK/Netherlands;
- preserves the April 1941 neutrality pact and Japanese diplomatic credibility after China peace;
- avoids a difficult fortified Far East campaign;
- permits continued industrial growth and military modernization.

The stronger Japan appears, the more Soviet forces it can pin without firing.

## 3. Annual gates

### 1941
Center branch: non-entry.

Japan does not discard a newly signed neutrality arrangement merely because Germany attacks the USSR. Northern preparations and intelligence increase, but China peace/trade benefits dominate.

### 1942 winter
Stalingrad/Winter Storm creates a real debate. Better German performance makes entry more attractive, but Soviet collapse is not demonstrated. Center branch remains non-entry.

### 1943 summer/autumn
This is the first genuine high-risk political gate.

Pro-entry argument:
- Germany remains militarily potent;
- Soviet armored losses are high;
- Japanese technical aid demonstrably matters;
- an eastern blow might divide Soviet reserves.

Anti-entry argument:
- Germany is still strategically retreating despite tactical successes;
- Far East Soviet defenses remain substantial;
- Japan already pins them cheaply;
- entry risks Anglo-American economic/diplomatic rupture;
- late-summer/early-autumn operational timing worsens quickly.

Center result:
- Japan raises northern war-readiness to a genuinely executable level;
- does not cross the border;
- Soviet intelligence detects preparation and retains additional forces.

### 1944
Entry remains open but no center-branch attack occurs through year-end.

The paradox strengthens:

> Germany is stronger, so intervention may matter more; but Japan is stronger, so non-belligerent deterrent/binding is also more valuable.

## 4. Minimum conditions for a future center-branch entry

A serious 1945+ entry should not occur merely because Tokyo likes Germany. At least several of the following should be met:

- the USSR suffers a new major army-level disaster;
- German operational initiative returns rather than mere orderly retreat;
- Soviet Far East readiness is demonstrably reduced or penetrable;
- Japanese leadership judges Anglo-American escalation tolerable/manageable;
- a limited war aim exists: Primorye/Vladivostok/Trans-Siberian interdiction rather than fantasy conquest to the Urals;
- expected duration is bounded enough to preserve Japan's economic position.

## 5. Next replay relevance

The 1945 German-offensive question and Japanese-entry question are coupled. A credible German operational offensive can raise Japanese entry probability. Conversely, visible Japanese preparation can force the USSR to retain eastern resources, improving German operational opportunities even if Japan never enters.
