# MATERIAL NO.3 — PRACTICAL HEAVY TRANSPORT ORIGIN — V8 AIRPOWER11

> **Current interpretation:** Material No.3 is not a third small material coupon aircraft and not an all-composite fighter. It is the point where the lower material-test ladder is connected to a useful very-large transport. Its internal material-program identity can later become the Nakajima/Asai AT-3 technical program and, only if the Army actually buys it, a Ki-92 evaluation/service lineage.
>
> No combat-clock advance and no future procurement result is created here.

## 1. Why a transport is the correct material outlet

Material No.1 answers low-speed structural/process questions. Material No.2 answers high-q structural questions. Neither pays the largest practical problem:

**can Asai's bonding, sandwich, large-panel, inspection and repair system scale from laboratory/one-off panels into a very large airframe whose size would otherwise make structural mass, part count, riveting labor and repair burden explode?**

A cargo transport is the best answer because it gives the material program a real economic/operational reason to be large:

- a large box produces large skin/panel/floor/door area where bonded/sandwich construction has leverage;
- 10-15 t high-value freight makes a low-rate fleet useful even before mass production;
- repeated loading, ramp use, rough-field vibration, weather exposure and repair create more useful structural-service data than a ceremonial giant test airplane;
- material mass saved can be spent directly on cargo or fuel rather than headline speed;
- cargo service can support a commercial/industrial lease loop and does not require a military combat doctrine to justify the airframe.

Thus **transport is not incidental to Material No.3; it is the mechanism that makes the large material demonstrator practical.**

## 4. First-generation material architecture

The first aircraft is **not** required to be primary-CFRP throughout. That would unnecessarily couple Material No.3 to the CFRP-I-IV qualification clock.

Primary/concentrated load paths initially remain metallic where that is the cleanest answer:

- center-wing carry-through and major wing bending members;
- engine mounts / firewall / hot zones;
- landing-gear attachment and concentrated floor/ramp hinges;
- local heavy cargo load tracks and fittings.

Large-area structure deliberately uses the material/process gains already being earned:

- bonded metal skins/stiffeners where they reduce holes/part count and improve fatigue behavior;
- paper-honeycomb and metal-face sandwich for large doors, floors, fairings and secondary shells;
- hemp/plant-FRP where low-cost noncritical area substitution is valuable;
- GFRP for selected wet/electrical/secondary areas as its release permits;
- CFRP local stiffeners/selected panels can enter only where CFRP-I/II evidence supports them; Material No.3 does not wait for an all-CFRP primary airframe.

This is the **upper-scale sibling** of the Material No.1/2 process school, not a replacement for CFRP-I-IV.

## 5. Material gain target

Before a flight article exists, treat material benefit as a design allocation, not measured fact.

For a 28-30 t operating-empty target, a useful first-generation program objective is roughly **5-8% empty-structure/equipped-mass relief versus an otherwise comparable all-riveted conventional large structure**, or about **1.5-2.5 t class** at whole-aircraft level after practical reinforcements and systems are paid.

The gain is not expected to come from one miracle material. It is the sum of:

- large smooth bonded panels and fewer fasteners/stiffeners;
- sandwich doors/floors/secondary shells;
- local high-specific-strength fittings/patches;
- lower corrosion/fatigue rework in selected structures;
- production tooling/process discipline that prevents large-aircraft weight growth through local redesign and uncontrolled tolerances.

The actual measured saving remains a future prototype result.
