## 1. What is retained and what is superseded

Retain from the old v48 CFRP program:

- a first-layer experimental sequence I-IV before a practical CFRP combat aircraft;
- identical-process **ground/flight twins** so process scatter can be separated from design error;
- Series I = skin/bond/galvanic isolation;
- Series II = CFRP primary spar/torsion-member qualification;
- Series III = thin-wing aeroelastic qualification;
- Series IV = high-Mach flight qualification;
- the program output is a design-allowables/inspection/repair book, not a publicity speed record.

Supersede as current ceilings:

- the old blanket statement that structural CFRP cannot become more than point reinforcement during the war;
- the old rule that the practical second-layer aircraft must stop at a straight-wing M0.77-0.78 Hakkou/Firen configuration;
- any timing derived merely by waiting for historical post-war carbon-fiber adoption.

The reincarnator knows the high-value route: oriented rayon precursor, controlled oxidation/carbonization/graphitization under tension, surface/interface treatment, epoxy matrix, galvanic isolation, fiber-direction stiffness design, fatigue/NDT and eventually swept/transonic composite aeroelasticity. Asai therefore pays these clocks deliberately and early.

## 2. Pre-paid material/process route by 1939

The current 1939 program is not starting from a laboratory discovery of carbon fiber. It inherits:

- rayon / cellulose chemistry and industrial fiber handling;
- high-temperature electric-furnace and controlled-atmosphere experience from metallurgy/GT work;
- future-known tensioned carbonization/graphitization as the intended structural-fiber route;
- in-house epoxy chemistry/bonding practice and sandwich/adhesive process control;
- glass fiber as an electrical/galvanic isolation layer where available;
- surface preparation, sealing, accelerated salt/humidity exposure;
- X-ray/ultrasonic/other NDT development and statistical lot/process control;
- precision winding, balancing, gauges, coupons and fatigue rigs.

What still has to be earned in real time is **repeatable structural allowables**: fiber/process scatter, interface durability, bonded-joint fatigue, galvanic isolation life, full-size stiffness, impact/handling damage, repair, and aeroelastic coefficients.

## 3. Common mother-airframe doctrine for Series I-III

Series I-III use a common dedicated structural-research mother airframe rather than three unrelated clean-sheet aircraft.

Working center:

- single seat;
- mature **E5-J** propulsion, normally the 425-455 kgf band rather than a new engine;
- conventional metal fuselage / engine bay / landing-gear hard points;
- side/cheek inlet architecture already paid by E5 future-form work;
- fuselage-mounted gear as far as practical so wing changes do not rewrite landing-gear load paths;
- rigid central carry-through and quickly replaceable research wings;
- standard flight instrumentation, with each wing having an identical-process full-scale ground twin.

The mother airframe is intentionally not a service fighter and does not need combat range or armament.

Useful planning coordinate (not a measured aircraft result):

- standard test weight roughly 1.7-1.9 t;
- wing area roughly 11-13 m2 depending research wing;
- airframe speed capability sufficient for the E5-J / controlled-dive regime of Series I-III;
- propulsion remains substantially fixed so structural changes are attributable.

## 4. Series I — skin / bonding / galvanic isolation

**Technical question:** can a CFRP skin/sandwich panel live on a metal aircraft without the interface, bond line or production scatter becoming the real failure mode?

Configuration:

- metal primary spar/carry-through;
- metal concentrated hard points;
- CFRP skin / sandwich panels over a deliberately conservative, relatively thick wing;
- glass isolation ply and sealed interface wherever CFRP approaches Al/Mg structure;
- suitable steel/stainless fittings where a local metallic load-transfer member is preferable;
- matched coupon, panel and complete-wing ground twins.

Measure:

- bond fatigue and peel/shear allowables;
- wet/hot and salt exposure;
- galvanic-current/corrosion rate at interface families;
- panel buckling/wrinkling;
- impact/handling damage detectability;
- scarf/patch repair repeatability;
- manufacturing scatter and NDT false-negative/false-positive rates.

### Timing

- 1938 H2-1939 Q1: precursor/interface/process coupons and full-size panels intensify;
- **1939-04-20:** full-scale Series-I wing/panel twins and corrosion/exposure racks are active; common mother-airframe detail design/jig work is active; no Series-I flight result is claimed;
- 1939 H2 / 1940 H1: first Series-I flight configuration is a technical target if ground gates pass;
- exposure/fatigue continues after first flight; first flight is not final qualification.

## 5. Series II — CFRP primary spar / torsion-member qualification

**Technical question:** can CFRP carry a real primary bending/torsion load path with predictable stiffness, fatigue and metal-fittings transfer?

Configuration:

- same mother-airframe logic;
- thick/safe aerodynamic wing so aerodynamic uncertainty remains small;
- CFRP D-spar / closed torsion member or equivalent wound primary member;
- metal root/carry-through and concentrated fittings retained initially;
- identical-process ground spar/wing twin driven through static and resonance/fatigue programs.

The reincarnator already knows fiber orientation is an engineering variable. Series II therefore does not spend years discovering that 0-degree fiber buys bending and +/-45-degree fiber buys shear/torsion; it spends time **measuring the actual allowables and joint/process scatter Asai can produce**.

### Timing target

- design work overlaps late Series I rather than waiting for Series I to finish completely;
- 1940: major Series-II ground articles and winding/process qualification;
- **1940 H2-1941 H1:** flight-wing gate if Series-I interface and Series-II structural twins pass;
- 1941 onward: repeated fatigue/repair/exposure continues and feeds Series III.

## 6. Series III — thin-wing CFRP aeroelastic qualification

**Technical question:** once CFRP is a primary structure, can a thin high-speed wing be designed to a predicted divergence/flutter/control-reversal boundary rather than merely survive static proof?

Configuration:

- CFRP primary spar/torsion box and skin system derived from Series II;
- thin straight research wing, initially around the old ~9-9.5% thickness family rather than jumping directly to the final transonic solution;
- exchangeable stiffness/lay-up variants, including deliberately softer ground/limited-flight articles to identify the aeroelastic model safely;
- pressure/load maps imported from HS-1/HS-2 and later HS-3 rather than guessed from incompressible theory.

Measure:

- bending/torsion coupling;
- divergence boundary;
- flutter damping and mode shape;
- aileron reversal / control effectiveness;
- shock-induced load migration once Mach is high enough;
- post-flight NDT correlation to measured strain/vibration history.

### Timing target

- 1941: design/ground-twin work can run while Series II flight/exposure continues;
- **1941 H2-1942:** first thin-wing flight qualification is technically reachable if the preceding gates remain healthy;
- exact Mach envelope is test-earned and must not be backfilled from the old Hakkou specification.

## 7. Series IV is now the HS/CFRP convergence step

Under the old program, Series IV mainly measured the tuck/buffet limit of the straight-wing Hakkou path. That endpoint is superseded by the AIRPOWER high-speed reconstruction.

Series IV now asks:

> can an actual CFRP thin primary wing survive and remain controllable in the transonic load/shock/aeroelastic environment already mapped by HS-3?

Therefore Series IV is no longer merely the final wing of the Series-I-III mother airframe. It transitions into the separate HS/CFRP joint vehicle closed in file 35.

## 9. What success would buy

The first-layer program earns specific freedoms:

- lighter primary structures without surrendering torsional stiffness;
- freedom to allocate stiffness by fiber direction rather than only by thickness;
- larger wing/fuel/mission volume for a given high-speed structural mass;
- fewer fastener holes and different fatigue/crack-growth behavior;
- a validated repair/inspection doctrine rather than an exotic one-off structure.

Those freedoms may later support a new Hakkou/Firen concept, a high-altitude/long-range platform, or another specialization. The **mission is deliberately not closed before Series IV and the high-speed program meet**.
