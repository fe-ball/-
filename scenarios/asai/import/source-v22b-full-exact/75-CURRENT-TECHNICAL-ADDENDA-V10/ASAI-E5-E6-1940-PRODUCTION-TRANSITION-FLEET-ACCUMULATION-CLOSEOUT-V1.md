## 1. 1939 E5 production ledger correction

The 1939-05-10 replay closed E5 at 98 cumulative completed aircraft (2 development + 96 military accepted). That value was later carried as a convenient physical reference, but it did not close the already-firm 1938 procurement balance.

At 1938-12-31 the firm E5 program basis was:
- 8 military evaluation aircraft;
- 36 first production aircraft;
- 24 exercised option aircraft;
- 72 second production tranche aircraft;
- **140 military airframes firm**, plus **2 development prototypes**.

By 1939-05-10, 96 military aircraft had been accepted. The remaining **44 military airframes** are not cancelled by any current gate, and the line had already demonstrated 16 completed aircraft in April 1939. Therefore the residual contract is closed as ordinary 1939 production rather than silently disappearing.

Residual completion center:

- June: +16;
- July: +18;
- **1939-07-31 cumulative: 142 total program aircraft = 2 development + 140 military accepted**.

No additional 1939 E5 airframe order is created by this closeout. Aircraft not explicitly allocated by theater remain home/training/reserve/maintenance pool. This correction does **not** add retroactive sorties, combat effects, or theater strength to already-closed battles.

## 3. Why E5 does not vanish when E6 starts

E6 is a large performance step, but E5 remains:
- cheaper by roughly 30% at mission-equipped aircraft level;
- fully mature in maintenance, fuel, drop-tank, mission-bay and field procedures;
- adequate for routine 0-400/500 km reconnaissance, BDA, courier, training, low-threat strike and secondary high-cover tasks;
- already present in quantity, so immediate replacement would waste useful airframes and training investment.

Therefore 1940 is a **production transition**, not an E5 scrapping program.

At the same time, there is little reason to preserve full-rate new E5 airframe construction after E6 service production stabilizes. Existing E5 stock is already large enough to absorb ordinary attrition while E6 takes premium missions.

## 5. 1940 ordinary-aircraft attrition planning

Neither family is lossless once it becomes ordinary service equipment.

For planning only, before 1940 combat replay:
- E5 military irreversible writeoffs: **12–18 for the year**, center **14**;
- E6 Twin irreversible writeoffs: **6–10 for the year**, center **8**.

These allowances include combat/ground-fire losses, damaged-return writeoffs, weather/landing accidents and ordinary service accidents. Exact causes, dates and theater attribution must be generated by the 1940 replay and must not be back-filled from this planning table.

E6 does not receive a fictional zero-loss bonus from speed. Early service aircraft also pay conversion, landing, acceptance and high-speed operating risk. Conversely, its speed should keep air-to-air irreversible loss unusually low unless doctrine is violated or the enemy obtains a favorable pre-positioned intercept.

## 8. Role transition through 1940

### Q1
E5 remains the numerical and doctrinal workhorse. E6 lead aircraft prove production standard, train cadres and begin selected operational work.

### Q2
E6 increasingly takes deep/high-threat reconnaissance, time-critical BDA, high-speed interception, premium escort/high-cover and long-range special missions. E5 shifts toward routine reconnaissance, courier, ordinary strike, training and lower-threat work.

### Q3
New E5 starts have effectively ended. E5 stock plateaus and begins natural decline if attrition exceeds tail completions. E6 becomes a normal service type with multiple-unit potential.

### Q4
Physical E5 and E6 service fleets approach parity. E5 remains numerous and useful; E6 owns the missions where speed/radius/survivability justify the ~30% acquisition premium.

## 9. 1941 implication

There is no automatic 1941 E5 new-airframe order. With ~150 E5 aircraft surviving at the end of 1940, depot rebuild and spares can sustain a substantial fleet even with zero new airframes. E5-equivalent engine/service products continue where economically useful.

If E6 remains at roughly 15+ accepted aircraft/month in 1941, E6 service extant naturally overtakes E5 during H1 1941 without scrapping E5. The transition is therefore **replacement by production share and attrition**, not administrative retirement.
