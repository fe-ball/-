# SOVIET-JAPANESE NEUTRALITY PACT GATE — NEXT CENTRAL TOPIC

## Exact treaty clock

Treaty signed: **1941-04-13**.  
Ratifications exchanged / pact entered into force: **1941-04-25**.  
Article III term: **five years from ratification**, therefore current term ends **1946-04-25**.  
If neither party denounces the pact **one year before expiration**, it automatically extends for another five years.  
Therefore the practical no-later-than denunciation deadline is **1945-04-25**.

Historical Soviet denunciation occurred 1945-04-05. Do not auto-import that event into the worldline.

Terminology guard: this is the **Soviet-Japanese Neutrality Pact**. It includes mutual neutrality if the other becomes the object of third-power military action; it is not merely a generic non-aggression pact.

## Why this worldline decision is much harder than history

Historical 1945 Soviet incentives were shaped by:
- a Japan already at war with the US/UK;
- Germany near defeat;
- Allied commitments to bring the USSR into the Pacific war;
- a Pacific-war endgame.

None of those conditions is automatically present here.

Current worldline conditions at 1944-12-31:
- Japan has honored armed neutrality for years and has not attacked the USSR;
- Japan is stronger economically/militarily than historical late-war Japan;
- Kwantung remains a credible threat and deterrent;
- Germany is losing territory but remains a functioning major military power;
- Soviet manpower/cadre strain is worse than historical branch;
- the US is still nonbelligerent toward both Japan and Germany;
- Britain wants Japan neutral if a US-German war begins;
- Soviet western war is not over and may continue deep into 1945 or beyond;
- letting the pact auto-extend would bind/shape Far-East options until 1951 unless later broken;
- denunciation would itself alarm Tokyo, potentially increasing Kwantung readiness and reducing Soviet ability to release Far-East forces during the still-active European war.

## Moscow decision tree

### Option S1 — Allow automatic extension
Benefits:
- maximizes confidence that Japan remains neutral;
- permits additional Far-East force release westward;
- reduces need for a second-front contingency during the decisive German war;
- supports Soviet claims that it is honoring treaties.

Costs:
- narrows legal/political freedom in the Far East through the immediate post-European-war period;
- Japan/Manchukuo remain strong and may become harder to pressure later;
- extension could frustrate future Soviet leverage over Manchuria, Korea, Sakhalin or regional security.

### Option S2 — Denounce but reassure
Benefits:
- preserves post-1946 freedom of action;
- does not necessarily imply immediate attack if Moscow publicly states current neutrality remains through the term.

Costs:
- Tokyo will treat denunciation as a major strategic warning;
- Kwantung demonstrations/readiness intensify;
- Japan may move closer to US/UK diplomatic accommodation;
- Soviet Far-East release may need to stop or reverse while Germany still fights.

### Option S3 — Seek renegotiated replacement
Possible form:
- a fresh neutrality/nonaggression arrangement with explicit longer-term borders/economic/fisheries clauses;
- perhaps implicit Japanese restraint in exchange for Soviet restraint and a clearer Manchukuo/Mongolia status formula.

Problem:
- both sides may demand a price that exposes their long-term intentions.

## Tokyo decision tree

Tokyo cannot force automatic extension if Moscow denounces, but it can shape the Soviet calculation.

### Option J1 — Quietly signal preference for extension
Risk: too much reassurance releases Soviet Far-East troops west.

### Option J2 — Remain deliberately ambiguous until close to deadline
Current HOLD logic favors this. Practical cooperation continues, but no unconditional long-term guarantee is offered.

### Option J3 — Japan itself denounces
This would be an extreme preventive-security move and would sharply raise war risk. At current clock there is no closed rationale for doing so.

### Option J4 — Offer a replacement bargain
Potentially trades stronger neutrality guarantees for economic/fisheries/security terms. This may be useful if Soviet denunciation risk becomes high.

## Information/perception problem

Moscow does not know whether Japanese periodic northern mobilization is genuine attack preparation or calibrated deterrence.

Tokyo does not know whether Soviet westbound rail movements are temporary German-war measures or the beginning of permanent confidence that Japan will never attack.

Both sides can therefore misread a treaty signal:
- Soviet denunciation as immediate war preparation;
- Japanese exercises as preemptive attack preparation;
- Japanese reassurance as weakness / permission to empty the Far East;
- Soviet silence as intent to auto-extend when Moscow may merely be delaying the decision.
