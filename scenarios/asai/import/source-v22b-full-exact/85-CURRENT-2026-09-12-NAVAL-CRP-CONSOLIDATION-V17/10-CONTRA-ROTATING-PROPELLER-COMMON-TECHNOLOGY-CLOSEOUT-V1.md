# Contra-rotating propeller (CRP) common-technology closeout

## Scope

This file supersedes the old one-way assumption that E15K CRP probably fails out and therefore N1K should begin biased toward a normal single-rotation propeller. It does **not** assert that CRP automatically succeeds. It establishes the national technical base and the qualification gates.

## Institutional model

### Sumitomo / propeller industry

Primary practical naval propeller/hub/pitch-control and manufacturing actor. Historical Hamilton/VDM lineage and production experience remain important.

### Mitsubishi / Nakajima and other engine makers

Own engine-side gearcase, torsional spectrum, lubrication and reduction-output conditions.

### Kawanishi / airframe firms

Own installation, water handling, cowling/float/airframe integration and real aircraft maintainability.

### Navy technical organizations

Own requirement, comparative evaluation, qualification and adoption decision.

### Asai

A major contributor but not national controller. Asai pursues CRP before E15K because its own turboprop roadmap predicts a future propeller power-absorption gate. It is especially strong in:

- high-load reduction gears;
- coaxial shafts;
- bearings and seals;
- lubrication and oil aeration measurement;
- torsional-vibration instrumentation;
- dynamic balancing;
- hydraulic governor/pitch-control testing;
- gauges, heat-treatment/process tracking and failure classification.

Asai does not become the default propeller manufacturer.

## Why CRP matters

CRP does not grant a huge free top-speed increase. Main benefits:

1. torque cancellation;
2. more power absorption at constrained diameter / tip speed;
3. partial swirl recovery and modest propulsive-efficiency gain.

This makes CRP especially valuable for high-powered single-float aircraft, carrier aircraft under diameter constraints, and later very-high-power turboprop products.

## Remaining failure map

Even with worldline materials/QC, the integrated system must still solve:

- reversing/planetary gear tooth fatigue;
- inner/outer coaxial-shaft bending + torsion;
- front/rear rotor torsional modes;
- thrust/translation-bearing heating;
- oil supply, foaming/aeration and return scavenging;
- shaft/hub seals;
- two-set pitch control response and synchronization;
- one-side pitch runaway / overspeed;
- blade mass/pitch matching;
- salt-water corrosion and maintenance access.

## Materials/process benefit

No magical material is required. Main worldline advantage is process control:

- surface-hardening/heat-treatment control;
- tooth grinding/contact inspection;
- bearing overlay/clearance discipline;
- shaft roundness/runout control;
- hub/blade-root fatigue inspection;
- lubrication temperature/pressure measurement;
- lot/gauge/acceptance traceability.

Composite propeller blades are not required for 1941–43 service CRP; metal blade/hub remains central.

## Maturity scale

- **CRP0** concept/analysis.
- **CRP1** component/rig: reversing gear, coaxial shaft, bearings, lubrication, hub and pitch mechanism.
- **CRP2** full-engine development: real power ground run; flight prototype allowed.
- **CRP3** installation qualified: specific engine+airframe passes overspeed, pitch change, vibration, water/takeoff/high-speed and environmental tests.
- **CRP4** serial reproducible: tooling, gauges, heat treatment, blade matching and acceptance standardized.

## Central worldline chronology

- 1936–37: Asai turboprop-backend concept studies compare large/slow prop, more blades, CRP and split power.
- 1937–38: low-power reversing-gear/coaxial-shaft/bearing rigs; independent propeller-industry research continues.
- 1938–39: split-power, hydraulic pitch and torsional rigs expand; still a technology option rather than service-aircraft program.
- **1939:** 14-Shi E15K becomes the first major Japanese aircraft project designed around CRP and creates a concrete joint-integration reason.
- 1940H1: Kasei-class CRP1→CRP2 work.
- 1940H2: ~1,400–1,500 hp full-power development candidate.
- 1941H1: E15K flight-development CRP2.
- 1941H2: successful E15K installation can reach CRP3; 1,800 hp growth rig runs in parallel.
- 1942H1–H2: 1,800–2,000 hp CRP3 and selected low-rate CRP4 become plausible if test results support them.
- 1942+: E7 turboprop CRP integration becomes a major backend option; this is the original long-term Asai motivation.

These are central planning chronology/gates, not guaranteed service dates.

## Application map

- E15K Shiun: A / very high value.
- N1K Kyofu: A / high value if E15K qualification succeeds.
- ordinary 1,800–2,200 hp land fighter: C; normal 4-blade often sufficient.
- carrier aircraft: B range depending diameter/landing-gear constraint.
- 2,250–2,650 shp turboprop: B+/A- candidate.
- 3,500–4,000 shp turboprop: A-class architecture option.
- ordinary twin-engine aircraft: lower torque-cancellation value because left/right rotation or other solutions exist.

## New E15K -> N1K rule

Old deterministic fallback is removed.

- If E15K reaches **CRP3** in useful time, N1K keeps CRP as a main candidate.
- If E15K remains stuck at CRP1/2 with unacceptable reliability/maintenance, N1K can revert to a normal propeller as historical-type risk reduction.

The outcome is test-gated, not predetermined.
