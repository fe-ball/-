# Branch B — New Caledonia fire-support / observation architecture close, 1942-07-30 v001

Status: **OPERATIONAL REQUIREMENT CLOSED / EXACT NAMED AIRCRAFT & BEACH GEOMETRY OPEN**
Clock: **1942-07-30 00:00**
Authority: v085 invasion/surface-response design + v071/v081 water-air lineage + recovered 1942 Japanese bombardment practice.

## 0. Core ruling

NC naval fire support is not a battleship-only function and is not resolved by an abstract bombardment bonus.
The effective chain is:

**target discovery → target identification/fix → observer/liaison assignment → fire-support ship assignment → first salvo → correction → target effect assessment → retask.**

The limiting quantity is therefore not only shell count. It is the number of **simultaneously maintainable observation channels** and the age/quality of each target track.

Branch computation/reporting reduces grid, plotting, ammunition-lot and handoff errors. It does not create a modern digital fire-support network.

## 1. Fire-support layers

### A. Heavy pre-landing fire — BB + CA
Primary targets:
- known coast/AA batteries;
- airfield revetments/runways/fuel/communications;
- road/bridge chokepoints;
- known artillery positions;
- fixed command/base installations.

Kongo-pair heavy fire remains a bounded pre-H mission. CA 20.3-cm fire is a major parallel layer, not an afterthought.

Historical calibration retained:
- Kongo/Haruna Henderson bombardment used floatplanes to mark the target area with flares and delivered 800+ 14-in rounds in one night;
- Myoko/Maya and later Maya/Suzuya-class cruiser bombardments delivered hundreds of 20-cm rounds and used floatplanes/flares.

NC center does not copy those round totals automatically; it uses them to establish that Japanese BB/CA shore fire can be intense when target marking and firing geometry are available.

### B. Close support — CA/CL/DD
After first-wave contact, responsive fire shifts down-caliber:
- visible MG/mortar positions;
- beach exits;
- roadblocks;
- artillery muzzle flashes;
- observed tank/vehicle assembly points;
- local infantry counterattack concentrations.

CL/DD can respond faster and remain nearer the landing system than a capital-ship pair that must preserve anti-surface readiness.

### C. Carrier / land-air strike layer
Carrier/land aircraft suppress:
- inland mobile reserves;
- road columns;
- airfields/AA;
- high-value targets beyond good naval line-of-sight.

Aircraft are not treated as continuously on-call anti-tank artillery.

### D. Ground artillery after landing
Once Japanese 75-mm/other guns and FOs are ashore, ground fire gradually replaces shipfire for danger-close/local targets.

## 2. Japanese aerial observation resources — planning envelope

Exact named NC task groups are still open, therefore these are **authorization requirements / derived envelopes**, not an executed roster.

### 2.1 Shipboard floatplanes — primary real-time naval-gunfire observer pool
With a four-Kongo system plus roughly 6–8 forward CAs and attached CLs, the fleet can plausibly assemble:
- nominal physical floatplane family: **~32–40**;
- serviceable: **~27–34**;
- mission-ready: **~24–30** center envelope.

This includes E13A/F1M-family observation/reconnaissance aircraft and any ship-specific equivalents. Tone/Chikuma-type aircraft capacity, if assigned, must be counted from the exact named OOB rather than added abstractly.

The fleet may not devote all of them to shore spotting. Search, ASW, surface contact, liaison and recovery duties remain.

### 2.2 D-day fire-support allocation from shipboard aircraft
Authorization center during roughly H-6 through H+8:
- **12–16 MR aircraft assigned to bombardment spotting / target marking / landing liaison**;
- **5–8 simultaneously useful observation channels over/near the target system** when weather/fighter interference is acceptable;
- remaining floatplanes retained for fleet search, surface-response warning, ASW/local liaison and relief.

Typical simultaneous split:
- main landing axis: **2–3 airborne observers/markers**;
- secondary axis: **1–2**;
- road/tank reinforcement corridor: **1–2**;
- one relief/communications/target-confirmation slot as available.

This is not 8 aircraft all observing the same battery. It is 5–8 separate useful channels over a dispersed NC battle area.

### 2.3 Sortie budget
Shipboard fire-support/liaison aircraft:
- D-day centered **18–26 launches/sorties** for spotting, target marking, liaison and battle-damage/recontact;
- a short surge toward ~28 is possible only by reducing other shipboard search/ASW roles and increasing maintenance/fatigue risk.

Aircraft loss/damage/abort immediately reduces later fire-support continuity.

## 3. Santo-based observation / reconnaissance

By an early-September NC operation, Santo has had more than a month to mature after the 29-Jul capture.
Existing gates permit a much stronger local ISR node than the 4-E13A improvised detachment of 29 Jul, but exact September maturation still requires the logistics/air audit.

### Planning requirement for NC
- water-air reconnaissance/observation available from Santo: **8–12 MR center requirement**;
- primarily E13A-class long-range aircraft; F1M-class aircraft are more useful locally and are not given a free ~400-nm each-way NC spotting mission;
- sustainable NC-directed sorties from Santo: roughly **6–10/day**, depending fuel, weather and maintenance.

### Role distinction
Santo aircraft are best used for:
- pre-H photographic/visual updates;
- identifying new road camps, airfield dispersal, shipping and possible battery positions;
- outer NC coastal search;
- post-contact re-acquisition;
- surface-fleet / reinforcement warning.

They are **not** the primary minute-to-minute BB/CA gunfire spotters. Santo→southern NC distance consumes too much transit/loiter margin for continuous close spotting. Shipboard aircraft and later ground observers carry that job.

## 4. Carrier fast-reconnaissance layer

D4Y/B5N-type carrier scouts are retained principally for:
- U.S. fast-BB/cruiser/carrier contact;
- fast confirmation of ambiguous surface contacts;
- occasional urgent inland target confirmation.

Do not spend scarce carrier reconnaissance aircraft on routine shell spotting when shipboard/Santo aviation can do it.

Carrier scout information can cue fire support, but a report must still be physically handed to the bombardment plot and may age before the ship can engage.

## 5. Ground observation / liaison after first touch

The first wave must include a deliberately priced observation/liaison package.

### Working requirement
- **10–14 ground fire-support observation / liaison teams** across the two initial axes;
- main axis: **6–8**;
- secondary axis: **3–4**;
- reserve/relay: **1–2**.

These are not U.S.-style late-war naval gunfire liaison teams magically copied into the IJA/IJN. They are Branch-planned packets built from:
- Army artillery forward observers;
- naval landing/base communications personnel;
- preassigned ship-to-shore radio channels/call signs;
- beach-control/signals teams;
- precomputed target grids and landmarks.

### Handoff latency center
If direct radio to an assigned support ship works:
- contact → usable fire mission: **~5–15 min** for a clear target/grid.

If relay through beach/ground HQ is required:
- **~15–30 min** center.

This is too slow for a tank that appears at 800 m and fires immediately. Organic AT/mines/guns remain mandatory.

## 6. Target freshness / what can actually be observed

### Fixed / pre-registered targets — HIGH observation confidence
Examples:
- airfield strips/revetments;
- port/fuel/warehouse compounds;
- known coast/AA batteries;
- bridges/major road junctions;
- large barracks/base compounds.

Fire can begin from pre-H photo/map registration. Air observation improves correction and damage assessment but is not required for every first salvo.

### Semi-fixed tactical targets — MEDIUM-HIGH when visible
Examples:
- newly firing artillery battery;
- roadblock/strongpoint;
- vehicle park;
- tank assembly area in open plantation/road terrain.

Usable track age:
- ideally **<10–15 min** for concentrated fire;
- **15–30 min** may still justify area fire if movement options are constrained;
- older than ~30 min without recontact is not a precise mobile-target solution.

### Mobile road columns / M3 tank packets — MEDIUM, episodic
If moving on an exposed road/corridor and an observer is already present:
- detection-to-report can be **~5–15 min**;
- ship-fire decision/handoff adds time;
- realistic naval-fire effect is road interdiction/area suppression, not guaranteed tank kills.

If tanks are dispersed under trees, behind terrain, or moved during rain/cloud:
- observation can fail entirely until ground contact or a new air pass.

### Close infantry targets — ground observation preferred
Inside roughly danger-close distances to Japanese troops, naval fire becomes slower/riskier. Ground guns, mortars and direct-fire weapons are preferred unless a clearly separated target can be marked.

## 7. Terrain and weather limits

NC's west/southwest road/plain corridors give useful observation opportunities, but the island's ridges, vegetation, plantations, folds and settlements create strong blind zones.

Center planning rule:
- coastal plain / road / airfield target visibility: generally favorable when weather is open;
- inland reverse slopes / wooded assembly: poor without repeated air/ground observation;
- tropical cloud/rain squalls can interrupt a planned observation window.

For planning, assume **~15–30% of nominal daylight aerial-observation time is degraded or temporarily broken** by cloud, squalls, fighter/AA pressure, navigation/communications friction or observer turnover. Do not reduce all fire accuracy by one multiplier; suspend/reduce the affected observation channel.

## 8. Night observation

Japanese floatplanes historically used parachute flares to illuminate/mark targets and ships.

NC night fire can therefore use:
- pre-registered fixed-target grids;
- flare/illumination aircraft;
- visible fires/muzzle flashes;
- coastal landmarks.

Working night allocation:
- **3–6 flare/illumination/liaison launches** in a major bombardment night if aircraft/sea state permit.

Night limitations:
- good for broad fixed-area bombardment and illumination;
- poor for precise moving inland targets;
- aircraft over target may reveal the existence/direction of the bombardment force;
- keep separate from the night surface-search requirement when U.S. BB/CA intervention is possible.

## 9. Observation under Allied fighter/AA pressure

Spotter aircraft are slow and vulnerable.
The Japanese plan therefore needs:
- local fighter cover over the fire-support box;
- relief observers;
- at least ~25% observer-sortie reserve/overlap;
- multiple independent radio/target-marking channels.

A U.S. fighter pulse that drives off two observers may temporarily blind one shore-fire sector even if no Japanese ship is damaged.

This is why fire-support aviation is a real allocation cost, not free accuracy.

## 10. Allied counter-observation

The Allies also possess observation advantages:
- ground observers already occupy the road/base system;
- NC radar/air warning can detect approaching raids/aircraft where coverage exists;
- PBY/RNZAF Hudson search can locate invasion/support groups;
- U.S. BB/CA floatplanes and shore aircraft can support counter-bombardment/landing-area attack;
- 754th/Americal can report beachhead locations and road movement through established communications.

Therefore Japanese bombardment ships firing repeatedly from a fixed box become progressively easier to localize for B-17/medium-bomber/submarine/night-surface response.

The Japanese advantage is strongest in a **short, scheduled heavy-fire window followed by displacement**, not static all-day gunnery.

## 11. D-day observation success gates

Before NC authorization, the fire-support plan should pass all of these:

1. **Pre-H target pack:** major airfields, batteries, road nodes and base installations photographed/mapped recently enough for first-fire registration.
2. **Shipboard observer pool:** at least **12 MR** available to fire-support/liaison after preserving fleet search/ASW.
3. **Simultaneous channels:** ability to sustain at least **5 useful observation channels** across the two landing axes and mobile-reserve corridors during the critical first hours.
4. **Ground teams:** at least **10 fire-support/liaison teams** physically in first/early waves with working radios.
5. **Relief:** enough observer aircraft/crews to maintain the system after 20–30% disruption/abort/loss.
6. **Fleet-contact reserve:** observation support must not consume the carrier/shipboard search capacity needed to detect an approaching U.S. fast-BB group.

Failure of gates 2–4 should force either a smaller landing concept, heavier organic ground artillery/AT, or a delay.

## 12. Center observation budget summary

For the critical D-day H-6 to H+8 window, before exact named OOB:

- shipboard floatplanes MR total envelope: **24–30**;
- allocated to shore fire/liaison: **12–16**;
- simultaneous useful observation channels: **5–8**;

- Santo long-range NC-directed ISR: **6–10 sorties/day** from an **8–12 MR** water-air requirement;

These are planning requirements / capacity bands. Exact numbers must be rebuilt after named BB/CA/CL assignment, Santo September air maturation, chosen beaches, weather and Allied fighter/AA audit.
