## 0. Core ruling

New Caledonia is not designed as a pure bait operation and not as a pure amphibious escort mission.

The operation must be profitable in three cases:

1. **U.S. heavy surface fleet does not intervene:** Japan lands and seizes/denies the Noumea–Tontouta–La Foa strategic system. This alone is sufficient strategic return.
2. **U.S. cruiser/DD force performs a raid:** Japan protects unloading first, attempts exit-line interception, and accepts a limited raid if the invasion remains on schedule rather than ruining the operation in a chase.
3. **U.S. fast battleships intervene:** the operation shifts into a prepared layered fleet-interception problem under Japanese submarine/search/air/torpedo geometry.

Thus the preferred formula is:

**take NC if the enemy declines battle; punish or mission-kill the enemy if it chooses to contest NC.**

The surface-response objective is an additional return, not a prerequisite for territorial success.

## 1. Why this architecture is institutionally plausible in Branch B

The enabler is not “computation makes officers rational.” The main physical/institutional difference is **depth of surviving ships, staffs and experienced commanders**:

- no historical Guadalcanal/Savo/Cape Esperance destroyer/cruiser attrition chain has yet occurred;
- MI permanently costs Akagi/Kaga and some surface hulls, but Hiryu/Soryu survive and experienced carrier/surface cadres remain materially thicker;
- Santo costs aircraft/aircrew and landing craft, but no Japanese capital/surface combat hull is lost there;
- Kondo, Mikawa, Tanaka and other surface-command structures remain available unless a later event removes them;
- the Navy can therefore staff several mission groups rather than assigning transport escort, bombardment, carrier cover and pursuit to one overloaded formation.

Branch computation/reporting helps deconflict routes, fuel, contact reports, fire plans and boat schedules. It does **not** create a fused modern battle network and is not the reason the force has enough command depth to split missions.

## 2. Strategic objective hierarchy

### Primary objective — territorial/LOC return
- seize or permanently deny the principal NC port/airfield/road complex;
- sever or heavily tax the U.S.–Australia communications line;
- turn Santo + NC into a forward reconnaissance/air/submarine geometry;
- compel Allied redistribution toward Fiji, NZ and Australia even if the U.S. fleet refuses battle.

### Secondary objective — fleet displacement/attrition
- prevent U.S. surface forces from destroying the transport/landing system;
- mission-kill or damage fast BB/cruiser forces that enter the intervention box;
- force U.S. destroyers, fuel and readiness into repeated escort/ASW/night-interception expenditure.

### Tertiary objective — deny “free hit-and-run” behavior
- do not have the transport direct-cover group abandon the transports to chase raiders;
- assign a separate interception/cutoff geometry on the most likely U.S. exit lanes;
- use submarine/air dawn recontact against raiders that remain in range;
- if contact ages and the landing remains healthy, **do not chase outside the Santo/NC air-search envelope merely for prestige.**

A U.S. raid that damages one ship but fails to disrupt beach throughput can be an Allied tactical success and still a Japanese operational loss-making raid.

## 3. Japanese mission-group architecture — roles closed, exact hull list open

### A. Carrier fleet-strike group — Nagumo/Third Fleet function
Purpose:
- fleet CAP;
- enemy fast-BB/carrier search confirmation and strike;
- offensive reserve;
- avoid daily routine fixed-target work that Santo/base air can perform.

Early-Sep candidate decks remain:
- Zuikaku;
- Shokaku after mission-usable repair gate;
- Ryujo as fighter-heavy support;
- Junyo as additional support/fleet capacity if concentrated;
- Hiryu conditional if its first-line work-up naturally aligns;
- Soryu excluded from early-Sep center.

### B. Transport/direct-cover group — Tanaka-type function
Purpose:
- keep transports and landing craft alive;
- smoke, zig-zag, ASW and immediate torpedo counterattack;
- **never become the primary pursuit formation.**

Working requirement before named close:
- one CL command ship;
- **6–8 DD direct-cover mission slots** center.

### C. Close bombardment / beach-support group
Purpose:
- pre-H fixed-target suppression;
- road exits, artillery/AA positions and identified tank assembly areas;
- short close-fire windows during the first landing cycles.

Conceptual fast-BB pair:
- two Kongo-class hulls in the first heavy shore-fire rotation.

Cruisers/DD provide the closer, more responsive support after the capital ships clear the immediate daylight exposure box.

### D. Heavy night-interception group
Purpose:
- fight U.S. cruiser/DD/fast-BB forces entering the transport/bombardment box;
- shape enemy maneuvers with Type 93 torpedo screens rather than straight battleship approach;
- remain AP/anti-surface ready while the shore-fire pair carries a larger fixed-target ammunition burden.

Conceptual fast-BB pair:
- the other two Kongo-class hulls.

### E. Exit/cutoff surface group
Purpose:
- make a U.S. night strike pay for withdrawal;
- position off the most likely south/east exit geometry rather than detaching the transport escort in pursuit;
- normally CA/DD-heavy rather than another capital-ship formation.

This group only attacks on a usable physical track. It does not know the enemy’s retreat route by hindsight.

### F. Distant decisive-battle reserve — conditional
Yamato/Mutsu-type main-body reserve is **prepared, not automatically committed**.

Release gate requires:
- credible concentration of U.S. fast battleships;
- sufficient tanker/support days;
- a plausible open-water engagement rather than routine shore fire;
- adequate screens.

Do not count their heavy-gun fire into the D-day bombardment budget.

## 4. Response branches

### Branch S0 — no U.S. major surface response
- heavy night/interception group remains cover/reserve;
- one or more short shore-fire windows become available;
- distant battle-line reserve stays north;
- primary measure of success is beach throughput and base seizure.

### Branch S1 — cruiser/DD hit-and-run
Japanese center response:
1. direct cover stays with transports;
2. cutoff group attempts exit contact;
3. carrier/shore search tries to maintain the track through dawn;
4. submarine line attacks exits/cripples when geometry allows;
5. if contact is lost and unloading remains on schedule, no prestige chase.

### Branch S2 — one/two U.S. fast BBs intervene
- shore-fire pair breaks or limits bombardment as needed;
- heavy night group assumes anti-surface priority;
- fleet carriers prioritize a fresh fast-BB/carrier contact over lower-value fixed targets;
- submarines and long-range search seek repeated fixes;
- distant heavy reserve release becomes eligible but not automatic.

### Branch S3 — U.S. surface force deliberately refuses close action
Japan does not attempt to force a chase toward Australia/Fiji solely to obtain a fleet battle. The NC territorial objective supplies the return.

## 5. Allied rational response — not assumed suicidal

The Allies understand that Japan may be inviting a surface fight.

Expected Allied doctrine after a physical invasion confirmation:
- shore air and submarines attack first where possible;
- fast BB/cruiser forces avoid predictable daylight station-keeping under unresolved Japanese carrier cover;
- night approach/interdiction and rapid withdrawal are more attractive;
- local radar and NC ground observers increase the value of short night fire/interception missions;
- Ghormley/Nimitz may still decline a close surface battle if Japanese carrier/search geometry is too favorable.

Therefore “NC invasion => guaranteed U.S. battle-line charge” is prohibited.

## 6. Success/failure accounting

Do not judge the naval layer only by ships sunk.

Japanese operational success can coexist with:
- one transport damaged;
- one night raid escaping;
- a surface engagement declined;
if the first-day combined-arms landing schedule remains intact.

Conversely, nominal naval superiority can fail the operation if:
- transport unloading is broken;
- landing craft losses collapse heavy-equipment flow;
- one/two key oilers are mission-killed and carrier/heavy cover must leave;
- support ships chase raiders and expose the assault body;
- the U.S. tank/artillery counterattack arrives before organic Japanese AT/artillery reaches the road exits.

## 7. Guardrails

- no deterministic U.S. reaction;
- no transport escort pursuit that leaves the transports undefended;
- no “one escaped raid = Japanese defeat” if the territorial operation remains profitable;
- no Yamato/Mutsu routine shore-bombardment credit;
- no carrier deck spent as a substitute for organic beach AT/artillery;
- no automatic Fiji/Samoa continuation after NC.
