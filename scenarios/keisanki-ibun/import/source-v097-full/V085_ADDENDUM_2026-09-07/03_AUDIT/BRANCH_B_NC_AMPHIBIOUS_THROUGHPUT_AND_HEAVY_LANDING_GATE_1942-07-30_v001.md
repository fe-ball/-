# Branch B — New Caledonia amphibious throughput / heavy-landing gate, 1942-07-30 v001

Status: **PLANNING REQUIREMENT CLOSED; PHYSICAL DONOR/NAMED BOAT WALLET OPEN**  
Clock: **1942-07-30 00:00**

Purpose: define what the Japanese NC landing system must physically accomplish before deciding whether an 8th-Tank-Regiment-scale armor package is useful. This gate applies even if Japan chooses an AT-heavy/no-regimental-tank plan.

## 0. Core ruling

The NC problem is not “how many tanks can Japan bring.” It is:

**can the amphibious system turn merchant-shipping headcount into a combined-arms force ashore before Americal/754th Tank Battalion can counterattack through the road network?**

Armor is one layer on top of this system. The following requirements remain even if Japanese tanks are minimal.

## 1. Physical landing-craft implications

Historical Japanese landing-craft load anchors:
- 14-m Daihatsu: about **70 men or 10–12 t cargo or one 7.5-t-class tank**;
- 17-m Daihatsu: about **120 men or 16.5 t cargo or one 15.6-t-class tank**.

Therefore:
- Ha-Go/light-tank-class vehicles can use 14-m heavy craft in principle;
- Chi-Ha/medium-tank-class vehicles require **17-m/heavy-equivalent craft or another physically closed heavy lighter system**;
- adding a medium-tank regiment changes the landing-craft wallet, not merely the ground OOB.

## 2. Required fleet-level craft envelope — NOT yet a claim of availability

For a ~55–60k campaign package with at least two real landing axes, working authorization requirement:

### Large ramped craft
- **physical target: ~55–65**;
- **H-hour serviceable target: ~48–56**;
- includes troop/cargo/gun/vehicle roles and an explicit repair/loss margin.

### Small powered craft / motor launches
- **physical target: ~30–40**;
- **H-hour serviceable target: ~26–34**;
- HQ/signals, light weapons, beach control, liaison, casualty/boat shuttle.

### Heavy/tank-capable craft
If medium tanks are included in a meaningful first-day counterattack reserve:
- **~12–18 serviceable 17-m/heavy-equivalent slots** is the planning requirement for the early landing system, or an equivalent explicitly named transport/lighter solution.

If no medium-tank regiment is committed:
- heavy-capable requirement can fall, but **6–10 heavy-equivalent slots** remain desirable for prime movers, engineer plant, heavier guns and critical vehicles.

These numbers are requirements, not proof that September availability exists.

## 3. Donor-cost rule

No craft are created because the NC operation is important.

The eventual wallet must name withdrawals from:
- DEI/Java occupation and coastal supply;
- Malaya/Singapore systems;
- Philippines/local transport;
- China or other Army/Navy landing-craft pools where physically plausible;
- Santo/Rabaul/Truk support.

Every transferred craft loses local service days and requires transit, crews, maintenance and loading arrangements.

## 4. First-wave composition

The first wave is deliberately **not** pure infantry headcount.

It must contain:
- assault infantry;
- beach-control engineers;
- signals/forward observers;
- MG and 60/81-mm mortar equivalents;
- anti-tank guns/teams;
- mines/demolition/road-obstacle teams;
- ammunition;
- initial direct-fire/field-gun loads where sea/beach conditions permit.

Working first-touch target with the above craft scale:
- roughly **3,000–4,000 physical personnel plus critical heavy-support loads** across the principal axes.

This is a planning target, not a guaranteed battle result.

## 5. Counterattack clock — the key NC difference

754th Tank Battalion should be treated as a road-mobile counterattack reserve, not a beach-wall tank park.

Japanese planning question:

**what can be made combat-ready ashore before a U.S. tank/infantry packet can move from the Tontouta/La Foa/Noumea road system to the selected landing sector?**

Required center sequence:

### H to H+2/3
- beachheads survive;
- organic AT/mines/mortars/signals established;
- beach exits identified and traffic control functioning.

### H+3 to H+6
- first field/75-mm and stronger AT/direct-fire layer enters action;
- prime movers and engineer tools begin clearing road/bridge exits;
- Japanese force must be able to stop a limited M3 counterattack **without waiting for carrier aircraft to arrive.**

### H+4 to H+8 — if Japanese tanks are committed
- tank packets land only after usable exits/assembly areas exist;
- tanks become mobile reserve/counter-counterattack force, not the first object placed on an uncleared beach.

Carrier/naval gunfire reduces the probability and mass of the U.S. counterattack. It does not replace this organic ground layer.

## 6. Fire-support coupling

### Pre-landing heavy fire
Independent historical/Branch fire-support audit remains useful:
- a Kongo-class pair can plan roughly **300–600 14-in rounds total**, center ~450, for a bounded heavy mission;
- unresolved U.S. fast-BB threat argues against loading/firing as if both ships were pure shore batteries;
- retain anti-surface ammunition/readiness.

### Close support
After the heavy pair clears or assumes anti-surface readiness:
- CA/CL/DD fire supports road exits, observed batteries, strongpoints and tank assembly areas;
- shipboard/water-air observers update targets where actual contact exists.

### Air support
Carrier/land air priorities include:
- local CAP;
- road reinforcement/tank assembly interdiction;
- fixed airfield/AA suppression;
- enemy surface contact.

Do not turn aircraft into continuously available on-call anti-tank artillery.

## 7. Landing geography rules

A good NC landing site is not merely “close to Noumea” or “easy surf.” It must score well on:

1. ramped-craft access and beach gradient;
2. heavy-craft unloading;
3. road/bridge exits within useful distance;
4. ability to channel/observe U.S. tank counterattack routes;
5. naval gunfire observation and maneuver room;
6. fighter/CAP/search geometry;
7. distance from the densest coast-artillery/AA/base-defense system;
8. ability to join a second landing axis without creating two isolated mini-campaigns.

Direct frontal Noumea assault is therefore not preselected.

## 8. Working throughput success gates

These are authorization goals, not preselected battle outcomes:

- **first touch:** ~3–4k plus critical support;
- **H+6:** a sustainable two-axis combined-arms lodgment, not merely headcount;
- **end D-day:** roughly **15–20k physical ashore/controlled landing movement** if losses/sea state remain bounded, with the majority of first-priority AT/artillery/engineer ammunition landed;
- **D+1:** enough road/port/airfield access to transition from beach shuttle toward a land logistics axis.

Failure to meet the heavy-support clock can make 20,000 men ashore less useful than a smaller force with guns, AT and engineering already integrated.

## 9. Abort / delay triggers for the amphibious system

Before the operation is executable, planning must include thresholds for:
- early major-transport mission kills;
- ~20–25% loss/mission-kill of the critical heavy landing-craft pool before road exits open;
- loss of the oiler/support state needed to keep carrier/heavy cover on station;
- inability to land organic AT/artillery before the estimated U.S. mobile-counterattack window;
- beach/road geography proving incompatible with the planned heavy-craft/tank package.

A U.S. surface raid is therefore dangerous primarily when it breaks these physical clocks, not merely because it scores a hit.
