# Branch B — Aleutians System Re-audit
## 1942-09 through 1943-05-20 evening
Version: v001 / 2026-09-10
Status: **current local authority**

## 1. Why this segment was reopened

The previous working Attu line treated the spring-1943 emergency convoys in detail but did not separately settle the **1942 autumn/winter construction-bulk problem**. That omission matters because Attu/Kiska performance is highly nonlinear once real lumber, cement, drainage material, engines/generators, landing craft, handling gear, and construction machinery arrive before the U.S. blockade becomes mature.

The current audit therefore starts from the **Aleutians-wide supply plan**, not from the 26 March 1943 Komandorski gate.

## 2. Theater concept by late 1942

Current center:

- **Kiska = forward main base / principal naval strongpoint.**
- **Attu = western secondary strongpoint, reserve stock base, airfield project, and Kuriles-facing reception point.**
- **Semichi/Shemya = satellite airfield/survey outpost, supplied locally from Attu when direct bulk unloading is impractical.**
- **Amchitka = reconnaissance/weather/hydrographic “tripwire” and limited cache/outpost, not a permanent fortress.**
- **Adak and eastward = no permanent Japanese outpost in the center.** Reconnaissance, submarine, seaplane, short raid, and demolition missions only.

This is a defensive/denial system, not an Alaska conquest plan.

## 3. Weather and ship-reliability rule

The Branch advantage is internal and partly invisible even to Japan itself.

It does **not** mean:
- perfect fog prediction;
- no storm losses;
- Ryujo or a merchant ship becoming a different hull;
- flight operations in sea states that physically exceed deck limits.

It **does** mean a higher rate of successful missions through:
- improved 12–24 h synoptic weather use;
- fewer machinery aborts and late arrivals;
- better convoy cohesion and navigation;
- better knowledge of when U.S. aviation is weather-limited while Japanese ships remain usable;
- better loading/unloading sequence and local craft support;
- faster recovery after damage or weather disruption.

Japanese commanders need not think they possess a revolutionary advantage. Operationally it appears as “this captain/ship can still make the run,” “the forecast was good enough,” and “fewer missions fail for internal reasons.”

## 4. 1942 autumn–winter effective bulk

Use **effective landed construction cycles**, not calls/arrivals.

Working center, Nov–Dec 1942:
- Kiska + Attu combined useful landed cargo: **13–17 kt**.
- Of this, fixed/base-forming construction and handling material: **4–6 kt**.
- Key construction categories include real lumber, cement, corrugated steel/pipe/drainage, generators, pumps, winches, workshops, tractors/towing gear, track/trolley material, spare parts, and landing/handling craft.

Approximate effective-cycle interpretation by 31 Dec:
- **Attu: ~3 cycles**.
- **Kiska: ~2–2.5 cycles**.
- **Semichi: 0.1–0.2 cycle equivalent** through small local delivery/survey formation rather than direct bulk ship unloading.
- **Amchitka: reconnaissance + tiny cache/tripwire only.**

These figures are working theater-accounting units, not historical cargo manifests.

## 5. Kiska effects

Kiska remains the best-developed base.

By early 1943 it has materially improved:
- dry storage and winter accommodation;
- harbor handling;
- distributed ammunition/fuel storage;
- roads/communications/power;
- seaplane operation and repair;
- AA readiness and repair.

The land airfield is still difficult to complete under Amchitka/Adak air pressure and does not become a reliable land-fighter field in the current center.

## 6. Attu effects

Attu changes more qualitatively because the historical base was much poorer.

By 1 Jan 1943 current center has:
- meaningful winter stores;
- real lumber/construction stock;
- better unloading craft/gear;
- dry storage and workshops;
- drainage/access work already under way;
- an airfield project that can continue even if a later ship is lost.

Thus later 1943 convoys become **completion/replenishment missions**, not the first time the island receives base-forming material.

## 7. Semichi / Shemya

The historical direct bulk problem remains physical: poor/no natural harbor, high surf, exposed landing.

Current response is not to pretend large transports can unload safely. Instead:
- bulk goes to Attu;
- small craft make shorter local runs when sea state permits;
- a small survey/weather/communications/airfield-preparation party is established.

No large operational airbase exists there by early 1943 in the current center.

## 8. Amchitka reconnaissance and tripwire

Historical Japanese reconnaissance/test-pitting is retained and expanded only modestly.

Current center:
- late 1942 hydrographic, weather, ground/airfield-site survey is completed to useful operational quality;
- the data are retained at Kiska and are useful for later raid/counterlanding planning;
- after U.S. reconnaissance activity is detected, a **small tripwire team (roughly a few dozen men)** is reinserted with radio, weather, demolition, and local caches;
- mission priority: observe -> report -> sabotage/delay -> withdraw/evade; not decisive defense.

The strategic value is early warning and pre-existing terrain/hydrographic knowledge, not permanent occupation.

## 9. Operation CROWBAR — Jan 1943

The U.S. still occupies Amchitka.

Branch differences:
- Japanese tripwire reports the landing on or near D-day rather than allowing a ~12-day discovery delay;
- early seaplane and submarine harassment begins sooner;
- no major Japanese surface counterattack in the center because the U.S. cruiser/DD screen and SG radar make the cost unattractive;
- one Kiska supply run that historical caution cancelled is more likely to continue through a bad-weather window.

Current result:
- Amchitka remains U.S.-held;
- fighter-strip limited operation is delayed only **~3–6 days**, center around **20–22 Feb 1943**;
- Japanese early warning/harassment buys time but does not stop U.S. base construction;
- approximately **0.8–1.5 kt** additional useful high-value cargo reaches Kiska/Attu before Amchitka air pressure fully matures, relative to the weaker historical/old working line.

## 10. Feb–Mar blockade adaptation

The blockade becomes real. Branch advantages do not cancel SG radar or U.S. air superiority.

Current rulings:
- **Akagane Maru loss is retained** as a useful negative check: weather/reliability improvements do not save every single-merchant run through radar surface patrol.
- Japan shifts toward stronger escort, selected faster ships, better machinery states, and weather-window penetration.

### 10 March Attu convoy

Current center:
- convoy reaches Attu;
- historical short unloading is improved by pre-existing local craft/handling infrastructure;
- **effective landed cargo ~0.8–1.2 kt** center, including airfield/AA/fuel/ammunition/engineering value.

### 26–28 March convoy / Komandorski gate

Current center retains the old no-contact branch but with stronger causal support from the continuous logistics audit:
- slower/less suitable ship is not allowed to dictate the entire formation;
- convoy speed/cohesion and machinery state are better;
- U.S. high-grade COMINT cueing remains degraded by Branch COMSEC;
- the historical geometry is therefore not automatic.

Working contact probability remains **~30–45%**; current center is **no decisive Komandorski engagement**.

27–28 March:
- convoy reaches Attu;
- Yamazaki element ~550 men arrives;
- **effective landed cargo ~1.0–1.5 kt** center;
- cargo priority shifts toward ammunition, AA, aviation fuel, airfield completion, power/communications, repair, medical and engineer material rather than famine relief.

## 11. 31 March current state

### Attu
- physical strength: **~3,150–3,350**.
- combat effective: **~3,000–3,150**.
- food/ammunition: not in immediate crisis.
- airfield: nearing limited operational use.
- stock depth: materially greater than U.S. estimates.

### Kiska
- strength: **~5,000–5,800** center.
- strong harbor/seaplane/AA base.
- land airfield still under severe U.S. air-pressure constraint.

## 12. Attu airfield and April 1943

Current center:
- limited land-fighter operation begins **~5–10 Apr**;
- field is not all-weather and is repeatedly closed by mud, damage, and weather;
- Japanese repair capacity is much better than in the old line because stock and construction depth already exist.

U.S. reconnaissance can see the airfield and later the fighters, but cannot reliably infer the true depth of dispersed stocks.

## 13. U.S. intelligence and LANDCRAB

### 10 March working U.S. estimate
- garrison center: **~1,300–1,800**;
- high side: **~2,000–2,500**;
- airfield: incomplete but increasingly important;
- major error: assumes continuing blockade implies shallow stocks.

### mid/late April update
- garrison estimate rises to **~2,700–3,200**;
- limited fighter-field operation is recognized;
- AA/fortification estimate rises;
- **stock depth and recovery capacity remain underestimated**.

LANDCRAB is not cancelled. The political/strategic logic becomes:
> Attu is harder than expected, therefore it must be taken before the airbase matures further.

U.S. adaptation is mainly:
- better combat loading;
- more ready 105-mm ammunition and engineering/medical stores;
- faster reserve commitment;
- stronger surface-cover readiness.

It is not a wholesale 1944-style amphibious rebuild.

## 14. LANDCRAB — 11 May onward, pre-counterlanding center

- D-day remains **11 May 1943**.
- U.S. lodgments are established.
- Japanese beach-defense concept remains depth/ridge defense rather than wasteful surf-line defense.
- Attu land fighters and radar complicate Nassau/P-38 support but do not reverse U.S. air superiority.
- Japanese progress-denial is stronger; U.S. north/south convergence is delayed relative to history.

The detailed weapon/air OOB is in the May-11 snapshot file.

## 15. Carrier intervention and 16 May strike

Branch pilot-pool treatment is critical:
- do not discount selected Ryujo/Zuiho strike crews merely because the carriers are categorized as second-line/training/work-up platforms;
- pilot rotation/cellularization and avoided historical attrition preserve experienced strike leaders and competent crews;
- apply penalties to deck limits/weather/new-airframe readiness instead.

First carrier echelon center:
- **Ryujo + Zuiho**;
- ~60 practical aircraft total;
- 36 fighters / 24 mature Branch B5N torpedo aircraft;
- ~54–57 mission-ready before severe weather attrition;
- fighter force is predominantly late-Zuisei Zero generation.

16 May current center:
- first carrier torpedo strike + separate/lagged Paramushiro G4M strike reach the Massacre Bay shipping problem;
- one large U.S. transport is heavily damaged/mission-killed center;
- a second large transport is damaged center;
- landing-craft losses and air-alert interruption reduce the planned D+5 combat-stock accumulation;
- Japanese aircraft losses are material but not catastrophic.

U.S. lodgment is not starved. The primary effect is to push heavy naval support/CAP attention away from continuous shore support and to slow ground convergence.

## 16. Second carrier echelon

Current center by ~18–19 May:
- **Junyo + Hiyo** approach/join the North-Pacific operating system;
- working practical air group: 48 aircraft per ship center (21 fighter, 18 D3A-family dive bomber, 9 Branch B5N), new D4Y/B6N concentrated elsewhere in the center;
- combined first+second echelon mission-ready pool reaches roughly **~140 aircraft** after first-echelon losses/weather.

This creates a temporary local Japanese carrier-air superiority window because Branch U.S. fast-carrier recovery is still limited in May 1943.

## 17. First counterlanding — night 19/20 May

This is detailed separately in the counterlanding file. Current center:
- Japanese local sea-control window is achieved for one night;
- Massacre Bay is bombarded by heavy cruisers;
- **~1,500–1,750 Japanese troops** and **~120–180 t** high-value combat cargo land at Chichagof;
- U.S. outer LOC to Adak is **not** severed;
- Japanese sea control is local and temporary, not theater-wide.

## 18. 20 May daytime center

Actual historical weather strongly limits U.S. air support. In the Branch center:
- Japanese carrier air focuses mainly on sea denial/CAP rather than large CAS;
- U.S. heavy naval support remains cautious at the edge of the Japanese carrier threat;
- newly landed Japanese troops backfill Chichagof/Sarana/Fish Hook sectors, freeing terrain-experienced veterans for a local counterattack;
- Japanese counterattack strength: **~1,300–1,700** focused in the Clevesy/Jarmin axis;
- current center: U.S. historical breakthrough/linkup is blocked or partially reversed; stable north/south linkage is delayed at least another 1–2 days.

This is **not yet** a U.S. encirclement/annihilation result.
