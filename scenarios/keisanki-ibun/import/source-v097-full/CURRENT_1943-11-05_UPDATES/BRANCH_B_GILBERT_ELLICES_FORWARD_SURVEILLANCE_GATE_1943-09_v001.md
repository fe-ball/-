# Branch B — Gilbert/Ellice Forward Surveillance and Detection Gate, Sep 1943 v001

Status: CURRENT WORKING ISR SETTLEMENT
Clock: 1943-09-01 to 1943-09-18

## 1. Governing principle
Japan does not try to search every square mile of the Central Pacific.
It watches high-value signatures and corridors, then shifts scarce H8K, floatplane and submarine effort toward the sectors that become suspicious.

The surveillance stack is:
1. fixed-base search from Gilberts/Marshalls/Nauru/Wake/Midway as geometry permits;
2. H8K long-range reconnaissance and repeat-look doctrine;
3. submarine observation boxes near known Allied forward bases and likely transit lanes;
4. low/mid-grade traffic analysis: harbor, rescue, weather, fuel, photo, engineering and construction traffic;
5. occasional temporary low-footprint observation/listening parties on remote unoccupied islets only where insertion/resupply risk is acceptable;
6. rapid retasking after a suspicious contact rather than maintaining uniform patrol density.

## 2. Historical physical trigger retained
Historical U.S. Central-Pacific staging creates unavoidable signatures:
- Funafuti is already a known U.S. bomber/fleet anchorage.
- Nukufetau and Nanumea are occupied in Aug 1943.
- Baker is reoccupied on 1 Sep 1943; an airfield is operational by 11 Sep.
- Princeton and Belleau Wood cover Baker for roughly two weeks before joining Lexington for the 18–19 Sep Gilbert raid.
- A Japanese H8K historically reaches the Baker operation on 1 Sep but is shot down before reporting.

Branch implication:
A single scout can fail, but repeated H8K/submarine/traffic-analysis attempts make complete concealment of Baker activation improbable.

## 3. Japanese forward-watch method

### Deep fixed eyes
- Tarawa/Makin/Nauru/Jaluit/Mili/Taroa/Wotje/Kwajalein network watches the south/east approaches.
- H8K sectors are not permanently maximal; they expand when Ellice/Phoenix traffic rises.
- water-air detachments allow search to continue even if a runway node is temporarily suppressed.

### Submarine snooping
Submarines are not only attack platforms.
When an operation is suspected, selected boats are ordered to:
- observe approaches to Baker/Funafuti/Canton without forcing an attack;
- count shipping classes, CAP frequency and flight activity;
- report convoy/fast-warship direction;
- move later into likely carrier withdrawal or invasion approach boxes.

This is sparse and risky; one or two boats cannot continuously close the ocean.

### Thin tripwires
Small temporary observation/radio parties may be inserted on genuinely unoccupied peripheral islets where:
- concealment is good;
- local Allied patrol density is low;
- a submarine or small craft can recover them.
They are not planted inside active U.S. bases and are not assumed to survive indefinitely.
Their value is binary warning: unusual ship/air traffic, not precise OOB.

### Traffic history
Japanese stations compare weeks of:
- airfield construction traffic;
- rescue/weather/harbor signals;
- photographic/reconnaissance mission patterns;
- aviation-fuel and maintenance traffic;
- increased CAP/fighter-director activity against snoopers;
- then sudden radio silence.
This detects preparation patterns even without reading high-grade U.S. operational codes.

## 4. Detection bands for the Sep 1943 raid

### Baker / Ellice buildup
By 2–4 Sep:
- probability Japan correctly assesses Baker is being activated as a U.S. forward position: 70–85% center.
- exact troop/aircraft strength: low-to-medium confidence.

By 10–12 Sep:
- probability Japan knows Baker is operational or nearly operational and that Ellice/Phoenix forward activity has materially increased: 90%+.
- Princeton/Belleau Wood or at least carrier/CVL-class cover being present in the area: 55–70% center; exact identities uncertain.

### Operation-level warning
By 13–15 Sep:
- Central-Pacific carrier operation within days: 70–85%.
- Gilbert/Marshall southeastern axis: 65–80%.
- raid rather than invasion: 55–70%, because no large slow amphibious/landing-craft signature is detected.

By 16–17 Sep:
- Gilberts as primary raid zone: 75–85%.
- Tarawa/Makin/Abemama specific target set: 45–60%.
- exact carrier count: low-to-medium confidence.
- exact strike time/approach bearing: low confidence.

### Tactical warning on 18 Sep
Center:
- 55–70% chance that at least one H8K/submarine/forward cue produces 1–3 hours of useful warning before the main strike;
- 20–30% chance that U.S. CAP kills or drives off the key scout and warning collapses to local radar/visual 30–60 min;
- 10–15% chance Japan gets a fresh enough carrier-group contact to meaningfully cue submarines/long-range strike planning, but still not a stable weapon-quality carrier track.

## 5. U.S. countermeasures
The U.S. recognizes the exposure:
- disperse activity across Baker/Funafuti/Canton/Nanumea/Nukufetau;
- move fast groups at night and under radio silence;
- keep CAP/fighter direction specifically against H8K snoopers;
- avoid keeping the full carrier group at the same forward base;
- use at-sea rendezvous and late concentration;
- allow Japanese observers to see construction activity while hiding exact strike timing.

Therefore Japan can often infer *what kind of operation is coming and from which axis* before it can determine *the exact carrier position and H-hour*.

## 6. Strategic consequence
This ISR system does not let Japan ambush every U.S. raid.
It does three more realistic things:
- reduces the frequency of complete strategic surprise;
- lets outer bases enter RED/dispersal before attack;
- moves submarines and reconnaissance toward the correct sector earlier.

The U.S. response is to spend CAP sorties, fighter-director attention, deception effort and fuel to kill or evade Japanese eyes.
That cost is itself part of the Branch outer-defense tax.
