## 2. Comparison with previous branches

### Previous branch (v050–v055, Nov 1942 NC line)
- Santo fell 25–29 Sep; NC landing centered 8 Nov.
- Japan waited long enough to restore four fleet carriers and a much heavier surface/support system.
- The Allies also had much longer to reinforce: by 11 Nov, NC total personnel was ~50–56k; initial direct ground-combat pool ~34–39k.
- By 11 Nov 18:00 Japan had ~32–34k ashore and had already taken ~2.75–3.7k ground/landing K/W/MIA, while the line was still ~5.5–8.5 km north of Tontouta. Tontouta fell 12 Nov in the revised line.
- The naval war escalated into major radar/night battles: old v055 retains first-night heavy action and a second-night Yamato/Mutsu versus Washington/South Dakota exchange. A carrier battle also existed on the 14 Nov line.
- The branch was superseded before a final Noumea/cleanup settlement. It was clearly a much more expensive contest despite Japan's larger recovered force.

### Previous-previous branch (v048 authoritative Oct 1942 settlement)
- Air campaign 4–8 Oct; Wasp strike 9 Oct; landing 10 Oct.
- Allied center around late Sep: ~46–52k total, ~31–36k direct combat.
- Japanese campaign package: ~57–62k; four fleet carriers plus support air.
- Tontouta fell 16 Oct; Noumea major districts 24 Oct; cleanup ran to 19 Nov.
- Japan by 24 Oct: 10–13k ground casualties (KIA/MIA 3.0–3.8k), 60–75 aircraft lost, 3 transports sunk + 4–6 damaged, one DD sunk, Haruna medium-heavy damage.
- Wasp was damaged and forced out; Washington night action prevented a clean Japanese naval result.
- This branch already corrected an even older, understrength 25–35k Allied-defense assumption.

### Current branch significance
- Japan attacks earlier with less restored carrier/shore-air mass than either older line.
- The Allies have less time to fortify and concentrate, and the key U.S. heavy-fleet timing is unfavorable: South Dakota is grounded, Washington has not yet entered the theater cell, and Wasp only returns as the evacuation phase begins.
- Japan's D-day design is explicitly built around the resident 754th Tank Battalion threat, so AT/artillery/engineers are landed before rifle headcount maximization.
- Result: quicker Tontouta/Noumea seizure and no mandatory decisive fleet battle.
- Cost of this cheaper territorial victory: much of the Americal/Marine cadre and the U.S. fleet survives. Japan receives the place, not a destroyed South-Pacific U.S. field force.

## 3. Old transport-burden model — reusable components

The old 1942H2 strategic settlement used the following steady-state delivered-cargo centers:
- Midway: 10–15k t/month
- Port Moresby: 20–25k t/month
- Lunga/Tulagi: 15–20k t/month
- Santo: 8–12k t/month
- New Caledonia: 20–25k t/month
- other northern forward nodes: several k t/month
- overlap-adjusted forward-base network: ~75–95k t/month steady-state
- immediate post-campaign/reconstruction spike: previously ~90–120k t/month across the network
- steady ship-day tie: ~25–35 cargo ships plus tanker/escort capacity

This architecture remains usable because the geography, base set, voyage distances and broad garrison/air-base functions remain. It should not be discarded merely because NC fell earlier.

## 4. Current-branch corrections to the transport model

### New Caledonia
- Old steady 20–25k t/month remains a good center after stabilization if a division-scale garrison plus aviation/base units remains.
- First 4–6 weeks after 18 Sep should be higher: roughly 25–30k t/month equivalent is a better working center for ammunition/fuel replacement, port/airfield repair, engineers and large residual ground forces.
- If one of 2nd/38th Divisions is later released and northern resistance collapses, NC can move back toward the old 20–25k band rather than remaining at reconstruction peak.

### Whole forward-base network
- Old steady 75–95k t/month remains useful.
- Because Santo and NC are both active earlier, the temporary post-NC spike occurs in late Sep–Oct rather than November; working network peak ~90–110k t/month is adequate unless a new fleet battle or Fiji operation creates another surge.

### Assault concentration release
Old settlement: end of Limited-FS released roughly:
- 15–22 large transports from assault concentration;
- 3–5 tanker/oiler concentration slots;
- about 80–110k GRT-class military transport capacity for continental/Five-Go buildup.

Current ruling:
- retain 80–110k GRT as a **gross assault-concentration release envelope**, not as net new national merchant tonnage;
- current campaign has lower Japanese transport/naval loss and ends its strategic phase much earlier, so release can begin in late September and mature during October rather than after 20 November;
- some landing craft, engineers, base ships and garrison shipping remain tied to NC/Santo and are not part of the released capacity;
- the 8th Tank Regiment, if the current transfer is retained, is physically deducted from Five-Go even though transport capacity returns.

A prudent timing center:
- late Sep: partial release after first convoy cycle / wounded and excess troops lift;
- first half Oct: most large assault transports leave the NC concentration;
- mid/late Oct: old 80–110k GRT gross reallocation envelope can be fully realized if Fiji/Samoa remains stopped and no new emergency intervenes.

## 5. Backhaul mineral cargo

Old NC settlement used:
- Nov–Dec 1942: 2–4k t/month
- 1943Q1: 5–8k t/month
- spring/summer 1943: 7–10k t/month
- favorable ceiling: 10–12k t/month

The logic remains valid: mineral exports do not cancel southbound military supply; they reduce northbound empty running.

Current capture is earlier and port destruction is incomplete, so a small October pilot backhaul is plausible. Do not mechanically move the entire old ramp two months earlier because mine administration, labor, machinery, power and political settlement still require repair/reorganization.

Working correction:
- Oct: pilot 1–3k t/month equivalent if port/mines permit;
- Nov–Dec: ~3–5k t/month center;
- Q1 1943: old 5–8k center remains usable.

## 6. Strategic interpretation

Current NC is operationally cheaper than either previous branch but it is not a free strategic gain.

Japan saves:
- capital ships and most surface force;
- transport hulls;
- carrier decks from a decisive battle;
- weeks of ground combat;
- roughly six-to-eight weeks of assault-shipping concentration compared with the old October/November lines.

Japan newly pays:
- permanent/semipermanent supply of NC at roughly division/base-system scale;
- port/airfield restoration and AA/engineer/base construction;
- convoy escort and submarine-defense ship-days on a longer southern line;
- at least one division-scale occupation/security requirement until northern residual forces cease to matter;
- the Five-Go armor subtraction if 8th Tank Regiment remains committed south.

The main strategic dividend is therefore not 'NC is cheap to hold.' It is:
**NC was cheap to acquire relative to the older branches, and the absence of a Fiji/Samoa continuation releases the temporary assault concentration early enough to strengthen Five-Go preparation while the permanent NC supply bill remains.**
