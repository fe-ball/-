## 1. Historical baseline that must NOT be counted as Branch gain

The historical Indian Ocean was already an active ASW/escort theater.

1943 examples:
- Operation PAMPHLET (Feb 1943) received powerful Eastern Fleet cover, including Warspite, Resolution, Revenge, cruiser and destroyer support.
- The Eastern Fleet created pooled Arabian/Bengal/Ceylon escort arrangements in May.
- By mid-Sep 1943, expected German U-boat arrival caused regular convoy systems on Durban–Kilindini–Aden, Aden–Bombay and Colombo–Bombay routes, plus air-covered approach positions.
- In Oct 1943, rising west-India submarine pressure forced the BM/MB convoy escort from two ships to three, using escorts withdrawn from the East Coast group.
- Historical Oct 1943 U-boat activity alone sank six ships totaling 25,833 GRT and damaged three.
- Historical Nov 1943 full-month figure was another three ships / 21,869 GRT; only a fraction lies before the present 5-Nov cutoff.

Therefore:
**convoying, escort diversion, and tens of thousands of GRT sunk in late 1943 are historical baseline, not Branch-only effects.**

## 5. Major surface-force / carrier binding

### Important qualitative correction
Historical Eastern Fleet itself had no fleet carrier from roughly Jan to Oct 1943.
Therefore Branch losses of Indomitable and Formidable do **not** mean “two extra carriers are pinned in the Indian Ocean.”

They mean:
**the Royal Navy has no modern carrier elasticity with which to solve an Indian-Ocean crisis.**

That is a nonlinear reserve loss.

Consequences:
- Victorious loan to the U.S. is harder/shorter/more conditional.
- Mediterranean/Home Fleet carrier rotations have less slack.
- An Indian-Ocean crisis must be paid mainly with land aviation, escorts, route management and old/heavy surface units.
- Britain cannot cheaply answer a Japanese surface sortie by simply adding a modern fleet carrier.

### Surface heavy-unit opportunity cost
Branch Japanese Southwest surface wallet remains credible without permanent fleet-carrier presence.

Working additional British readiness/retention burden:
**~7–13 heavy-unit-month equivalents**
weighted across old battleship / heavy-cruiser readiness and delayed release.

This is NOT seven extra battleships.
It means a combination such as:
- one cruiser held several extra months;
- old-battleship readiness maintained around high-value convoys;
- extra heavy-unit cover windows;
- delayed refit/release.

### Japanese surface activity
Current branch:
- no 1942 renewed Operation-B raid;
- no large 1943 Ceylon fleet raid;
- center allows roughly **two medium short surface sweeps/demonstrations** during the period, plus routine local escort/show-of-force activity;
- typical force: CA/CL 1–3 + DD 3–6, operating 2–4 days near the Bay/Andaman air-recon umbrella;
- no major merchant sinkings are prebooked from these sweeps.

Their main effect is uncertainty:
merchant sailing delay, air reconnaissance surge, escort concentration and Eastern Fleet readiness.

## 9. Judgment

The current branch does not win the Indian Ocean by tonnage.

It does something strategically subtler:

**it prevents the Allies from converting the Indian Ocean from a combat theater into a logistics freeway on the historical timetable.**

This consumes fewer Japanese premium assets than a second 1942 fleet raid and creates less spectacular direct loss, but it has better persistence and couples more strongly to China/CBI by late 1943.

The first major qualitative threshold is therefore not a merchant-tonnage number.
It is whether Britain can safely reduce the theater's escort / patrol / heavy-unit readiness while Japanese and German submarines still cycle through Penang and Japanese reconnaissance/surface forces remain credible.

As of 5 Nov 1943, the answer is **no**.
