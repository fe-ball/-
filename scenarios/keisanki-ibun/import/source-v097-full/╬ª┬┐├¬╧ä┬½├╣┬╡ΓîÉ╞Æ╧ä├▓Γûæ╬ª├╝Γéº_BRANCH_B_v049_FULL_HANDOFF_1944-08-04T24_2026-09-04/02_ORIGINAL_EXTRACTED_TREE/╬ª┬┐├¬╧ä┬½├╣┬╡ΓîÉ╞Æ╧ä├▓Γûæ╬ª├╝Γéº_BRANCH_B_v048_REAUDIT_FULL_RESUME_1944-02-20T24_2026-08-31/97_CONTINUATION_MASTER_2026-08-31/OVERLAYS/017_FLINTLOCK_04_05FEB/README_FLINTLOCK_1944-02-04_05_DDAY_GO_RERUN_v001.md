# FLINTLOCK 1944-02-04/05 integrated rerun and D-Day GO gate

Status: WORKING only. Continues the 1944-02-03T24 maritime rebaseline.

## Result
The 4-5 February interval is not copied from the older FLINTLOCK line. The US spends 4 February rebuilding a high-output suppression cycle after the 2 February carrier battle, then uses 5 February for repeated target reduction, lagoon/channel work, preliminary-islet preparation and assault-lane control. The Japanese carrier force does not return; local air, water-air and submarines remain the active maritime threat.

The US still selects **6 February** as the main assault day, but for newly rerun reasons. Five February is too early after the 3 February recovery day and a real submarine/night-air threat. Seven February wastes exposure time after Japanese runway-based air and primary target-island radar have already been driven to a small/intermittent remainder.

## Sea and air
On 4 February TF58 can restore about 155-185 offensive suppression sorties (center 170), plus roughly 52-66 relevant Gilbert-based sorties. On 5 February the fast-carrier suppression wallet rises to about 175-210 (center 192) plus 55-70 Gilbert-based sorties. CAP/search/ASW are additional and are not counted as attack sorties.

The seven existing old-battleship support slots carry the main heavy shore-fire burden. Most fast battleships remain in carrier/anti-surface cover through 4 February; increased confidence in Japanese withdrawal allows only limited redistribution toward intermediate cover. This avoids counting the same hull as carrier screen and close bombardment simultaneously.

The six CVE support groups remain important, but much of their useful work is CAP, ASW, spotting and preliminary-islet support. Tarawa learning creates more reserve and repeated reattack, not unlimited simultaneous throughput. Extra LVT/LST/engineer assets produce intermittent staging and lagoon-lane congestion during the preliminary phase, which is managed by separating gunfire, UDT and assault windows.

Marshall regional combat-serviceable air falls from the 3-Feb center 44 to **24-30, center 27** by 6-Feb dawn: roughly 11-14 fighters, 3-5 attack aircraft and 6-8 water/recon/other aircraft. The remaining water-air layer matters mainly for warning and cueing rather than mass strike.

Primary Roi/Kwajalein radar antennas are destroyed or intermittent centered after repeated reattack. Protected plot fragments, alternate power, phone routes, DF/radio, visual posts and water-air reports still provide general warning and shelter/remanning orders. This is a degraded component network, not an intact radar/GCI system.

## Submarine rerun
The superseded LST-224 event is **not** restored.

The Cabot cripple-hunt previously consumed 1-2 boat-days. By 4 February 9-11 Japanese boats have useful assault/support geometry. One northern approach to a CVE/fire-support screen is forced deep without firing.

On 5 February pre-dawn, a different conventional boat obtains a good solution on the southern assault/tractor corridor. Center result: **one unnamed LST-class landing ship receives one torpedo hit and is mission-killed for D-Day, but does not sink centered**. Exact hull and cargo remain open. Personnel casualties are roughly 30-65; some vehicles/stores are lost or inaccessible, but no named tank company is deleted without cargo trace. The attacker survives the immediate counterattack but withdraws mission-damaged.

A separate conventional submarine is sunk later on 5 February during another penetration attempt. Exact hull identity remains open. This is an independently rerun event, not inheritance of an old named loss.

A 6-8 aircraft Japanese night package on 4/5 February gains only 2-4 effective weapon runs and scores no ship hit; 2-3 aircraft are lost. A 2-4 aircraft harassment/recon package before 6 February also scores no hit.

## Ground, sensors, AA and vehicles at 6-Feb dawn
Tarawa lessons are physically present at Kwajalein/Roi-Namur: lagoon-side fire plans, local reserve routes, distributed ammunition/water, alternate command/telephone, registered mortar/artillery areas and crew shelters. There is still no later deep-cave doctrine on flat coral islands.

Working H-hour survival bands:
- sheltered infantry/mortar positions ready when fire lifts: 55-70%;
- coastal/direct-fire weapons physically fireable: 35-50%;
- those weapons simultaneously retaining useful observation/comms: 24-36%;
- heavy/medium AA physically fireable: 42-55%, but only 28-40% connected to useful observation/fire control;
- light AA physically fireable: 55-70%;
- key wire/obstacle routes still effective: 45-60%;
- forward ammunition usable: 60-75%.

Vehicle/armor tracing remains strict. No Japanese tank platoon is credited on Kwajalein or Roi. Namur receives only the previously traced minimum one Type 94 tankette, with 0-1 contact-capable at H-hour. Branch vehicle reliability/recovery improvements apply only to actual traced vehicles/prime movers and are sharply capped by tiny-island bombardment, wreck access and workshop survival.

The 5-Feb LST mission-kill reduces US landing/tractor reserve margin, but exact cargo is unknown and no US tank company is removed by fiat. The principal next question is therefore not whether the landing can occur, but how the strengthened defender, one-LST shortfall, increased LVT reserve, close-fire allocation and beach/lagoon traffic interact during the first 6-Feb waves.
