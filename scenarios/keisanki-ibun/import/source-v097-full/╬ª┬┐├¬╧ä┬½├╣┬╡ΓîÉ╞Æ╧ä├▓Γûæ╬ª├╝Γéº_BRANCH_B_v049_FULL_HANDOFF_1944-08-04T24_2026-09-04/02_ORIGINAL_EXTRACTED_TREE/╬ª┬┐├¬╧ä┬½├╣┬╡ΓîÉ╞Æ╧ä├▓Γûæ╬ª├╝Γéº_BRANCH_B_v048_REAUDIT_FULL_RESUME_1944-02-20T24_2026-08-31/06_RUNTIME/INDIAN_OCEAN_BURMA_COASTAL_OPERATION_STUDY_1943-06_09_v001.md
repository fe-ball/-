## 1. Strategic premise
This is not automatically a second fleet raid and not historical U-Go. The emerging concept is to pressure the CBI system from outside while Five-Go pressures China internally:
- Upper Assam/Hump/Ledo are observed and, if necessary, pressured;
- Arakan/Chittagong coast is threatened by a combined land-sea operation;
- Bay of Bengal shipping is taxed by submarines/limited surface and air activity;
- Addu is retained as a hidden sensor rather than attacked by default.

The operation must not depend on a mass Indian uprising or on the Eastern Fleet voluntarily accepting battle.

## 2. Allied capability premise
British interest in India/Burma is real, but historical reinforcement plans are not axioms. Branch Britain has additionally lost Indomitable and Formidable in 1942. The Eastern Fleet therefore lacks historical modern-carrier depth and cannot cheaply rescue a Bay of Bengal crisis by carrier force while Mediterranean/Home Fleet commitments remain.

Central 1943 summer British adaptation:
- prioritize India internal defense, fighters, AA, radar, engineers, rail/road and Assam airfields;
- maintain Hump/Ledo development;
- keep Chittagong defended;
- large Akyab/Ramree-style amphibious offensive is more conditional/expensive than historical planning;
- do not assume an impulsive British counteroffensive, but retain it as a low-probability branch if political/operational pressure creates it.

US central response to an India crisis:
- can add aircraft, engineers, radar, C-47/C-46, fuel/spares and China air support;
- is reluctant to transfer new fast carriers/fast BBs/large Pacific amphibious lift to the Bay;
- more likely indirect rescue is additional Pacific pressure forcing Japan to choose where its carriers remain.

## 3. Reconnaissance architecture
Three distinct layers:

### Upper Assam / Hump — Army reconnaissance
Myitkyina/Bhamo Ki-46-type reconnaissance can regularly observe Chabua, Dinjan, Mohanbari, Sookerating, Tinsukia/Ledo at operationally useful ranges. The purpose is not to count every Hump aircraft in flight but to track installed capacity and trend: hardstands, runway work, fuel areas, parked transports, road/rail traffic and fighter defense.

July 1943 Hump realized throughput is treated as roughly 3,500–4,500t/month depending accounting scope, below Allied aspirations but visibly expanding. Japanese inference: **not yet huge, but being built to become huge**.

### Bay / Chittagong — active sensor
Akyab/Port Blair reconnaissance plus occasional limited attacks on Chittagong measure warning time, CAP response, AA density, patrol and reinforcement cycle. Chittagong is suitable for `poke and observe` because its importance is already obvious to both sides; little hidden information is burned.

### Ceylon / Addu — Navy passive sensor
Addu location/role is essentially identified by branch mid-1942, but direct attack is avoided. Tanker/DD/flying-boat/traffic patterns and multiple indicators are used to estimate Eastern Fleet sortie probability. Single-indicator deterministic inference is prohibited.

## 4. Political / INA ledger
Bose/INA political value is real, but mass uprising is not a planning input.

Countable:
- INA regular units;
- INA Intelligence/Special Service/Bahadur-type personnel with defined tasks;
- verified two-way domestic contacts;
- specific Indian Army units/persons with evidence-based defection probability.

Not countable in base plan:
- generalized anti-British sentiment;
- Bengal famine anger;
- nationwide revolt;
- automatic Congress alignment.

Old contacts are retained with states such as VERIFIED / DORMANT-RECONTACT / COMPROMISED rather than discarded. Re-contact plans are legitimate because committed collaborators/contacts have operational value. A broad uprising is upside only.

Cox's Bazar land-sea link-up itself has political value: Japanese/INA forces visibly enter Indian territory and are supplied from the sea even if Chittagong is never captured.

## 5. Coastal logistics concept
Heavy spine:
`Rangoon/Singapore -> Akyab -> coastal small ship/barge -> Naf/Teknaf/Cox's -> Daihatsu/small craft final delivery`.

Do not send Daihatsu the entire Akyab-Cox's distance as the normal system. Large ships unload in rear areas; cargo is progressively fragmented into small nighttime coastal/river movements.

First Arakan geography supports water dependence: land communications south of Dohazari were poor, and later Allied development demonstrates high latent sea/river capacity. Do not back-cast later Allied 1,000t/day infrastructure into 1943 Japanese capability.

August exercise correction: Japanese forces do **not** already control Maungdaw/Teknaf/Cox's. The exercise occurs on Akyab/Mayu Japanese-side waters and simulates the future final leg. It produces engineering/handling evidence, not proof of enemy-front operation.

Working exercise output:
- first cycle calendar average ~190–230t/day;
- improved second cycle ~220–260t/day in controlled waters;
- weather still produces zero-output nights.

After applying enemy-front/air-risk penalty, Cox's system working estimate:
- local Japanese air superiority: 200–280t/day;
- contested air / night-heavy operation: **150–220t/day central**;
- clear Allied air superiority: 90–150t/day;
- continuous day/night surveillance + coastal-boat dominance: <100t/day, withdrawal/stop gate.

This overlaps a one-division reinforced defensive requirement but does not support a multi-division deep advance toward Calcutta.

## 6. Air-drop / flying-boat support
Airborne supply is explicitly **not a LOC axis**. Army transports and limited naval transport/flying-boat support may deliver small high-value emergency loads (ammunition, medical, radio batteries, engineers/specialists) for 24–72h shocks.

Planning rule: do not add air-drop tonnage to sea/land LOC to make the operation pass. If a formation cannot survive without persistent air supply, the advance is RED. Air delivery is a small field-supply insurance only.

## 7. Operational gates / objectives
Gate A: secure/approach Maungdaw–Naf land-sea connection.

Gate B: Cox's Bazar land-sea link-up. **Primary minimum success.** Army overland column and naval/amphibious/logistics element physically connect; forward coast can be supplied.

Gate C: limited pressure toward Dohazari/Sangu and disruption of Chittagong southern communications. Sangu can be a light lateral LOC, not a multi-division heavy spine.

Gate D: Chittagong capture only if defenses collapse or a favorable operational opportunity appears. Not mandatory.

If captured, Chittagong port capacity is planned as **0t/day for Japanese sustainment**. The value is to remove a unique Allied logistics node from the Allied network, while Japanese defenders remain supplied primarily from the southern coastal system. If Britain heavily fortifies Chittagong, Japan may achieve the objective by forcing that commitment without taking the city.

Do not continue automatically toward Calcutta/Assam. That recreates a long land LOC and destroys the operation's advantage.

## 8. Sustainment stop rules
Illustrative working thresholds for a reinforced-division forward position:
- >=240t/day 14-day mean: GREEN;
- 160–240: AMBER, offensive action prohibited / inventory draw controlled;
- <160t/day for 10–14 days: RED, planned withdrawal toward Cox's/Naf system.

Additional RED/AMBER indicators include sustained landing-craft monthly loss >10–15%, forward fighter readiness <50%, or Chittagong requiring >2 divisions to hold.

These are planning gates, not historical facts.

## 9. Naval concept
Main operation must work if Eastern Fleet does not sortie. Fleet destruction is an opportunity plan, not a prerequisite.

Central late-summer design trend:
- first-line carriers Shokaku/Zuikaku/Hiryu/Soryu remain Pacific;
- India operation is designed around Southwest Area Fleet, base air, submarines, secondary carriers if needed, old BBs/CA/DD, specialized landing ships and coastal escorts;
- if Eastern Fleet enters Bay to rescue the landing, only then transition to a pre-written capture/ambush plan;
- do not pursue to Addu/Ceylon just because the British retreat.
