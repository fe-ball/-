## 1. Sortie state and Nauru

Secondary Japanese carriers are not assumed to have been consumed by Bengal. The centered Bengal hull commitment is zero; Junyo/Hiyo/Ryujo/Zuiho spend the autumn as two-ocean reserve / routine ferry-patrol-training assets and move to Pacific priority as the US Central-Pacific threat becomes clear. Shoho/Ryuho remain visible background candidates but are not spawned into the immediate GALVANIC strike wallet without a dated readiness/location trace.

Nauru suppression is restored as a required precursor event. Wasp + Princeton strike Nauru on 11/23. Nauru is neither fake bait nor a first-priority base worth stripping Mili/Jaluit to save. It is a real, useful, lower-priority airfield that absorbs US carrier/land-air sorties while the main southern-Marshall/Gilberts reconnaissance and attack network is preserved. Japan takes the damage, maximizes warning reports and does not mount a major rescue diversion.

The Nauru attack and a separate Japanese dawn reconnaissance of another US carrier-cover group make the invasion scale legible before D-Day: Japan can infer at least two independent US carrier task groups, but cannot perfectly map all deck locations.

## 2. D-1 Independence strike

On 11/23 evening the healthier-than-historical Marshall air wallet produces about 22 attack sorties on the centered line. Roughly 7-9 aircraft reach useful torpedo-release geometry after US CAP/AA disruption.

USS Independence (CVL-22) takes one Type-91-class aerial torpedo around 17:55-18:10. She survives, stabilizes on reduced propulsion and becomes campaign-out. Japan loses roughly 13-15 aircraft, center about 14, including expensive night/torpedo-qualified crews.

This early strike is strategically double-edged: it removes a US flight deck before D-Day, but it also raises US anti-air/ASW/screen vigilance. The historical D+4 geometry that killed Liscome Bay therefore does not automatically recur on Branch D-Day.

## 3. Makin and Liscome Bay

Makin begins on 11/24 morning. USS Liscome Bay survives. I-175 can approach and threaten the screen, but the D-Day force is more concentrated and more alert than the historical temporary D+4 TG that exposed Liscome Bay.

Makin organized resistance ends on 11/27. CVE support remains intact throughout the decisive ground period.

## 4. Tarawa tide, landing and defender accounting

Because D-Day is 11/24 rather than historical 11/20, the historical reef/tide outcome cannot be copied. Both sides treat tide as an operational variable.

The US has tide tables, Holland's warning, reconnaissance and pilots, but not perfect reef-flat depth prediction. Japan has stronger local empirical knowledge of Betio reef, lanes and daily water behavior. The US therefore uses an observed-depth plan: LVT waves cross first; LCVP/LCM are released only through verified lanes as water rises.

Do not credit a voided 1943 'second Midway' amphibious lesson. Do not import the historical Guadalcanal veteran density into the Branch 2nd Marine Division. The Branch division is healthier and organizationally fresher, but materially less combat-hardened.

Tarawa defender accounting is:
- total personnel: about 4,900-5,300;
- first-line combat: about 2,600-2,900;
- limited armed-support pool: about 700-1,000;
- remaining construction, air-ground, labor and support personnel do not equal first-line SNLF infantry.

A limited Japanese daytime raid timed to the afternoon assault window is mostly defeated, but it disrupts final naval/air neutralization for roughly 15-25 minutes. The first three LVT waves therefore meet a comparatively synchronized surviving beach-fire system. Of the initial 87 LVT, roughly 12-16 are destroyed/mission-killed before or just after landing and more are damaged.

The crucial US compensation comes later: verified lanes and the 11/24 high-water window allow a substantial fraction of later LCVP/LCM to cross instead of forcing mass reef wading. Tanks, engineers, 75-mm pack howitzers, ammunition, water and dry radios reach the beach much earlier than historical 11/20.

## 5. First-night counterattack and ground closeout

Japan uses the first-night opportunity rather than wasting it. About 350-450 first-line troops plus 3-4 Type 95 tanks conduct an organized limited counterattack against the Red 2/3 connection and supply/command nodes. It gains local ground but is stopped by US artillery, Shermans, machine guns, illumination and destroyer fire. Japan then reverts to infiltration rather than spending the entire garrison in a banzai assault.

On 11/25-27 the US improvises and learns a tank + engineer + 75-mm + destroyer-direct-fire reduction cycle. Japanese local C2 remains somewhat better than historical, but the first-night attack has spent much of the mobile reserve. Green Beach and the beachhead connection are secured; defense breaks into east/north strongpoint pockets.

Organized resistance ends 11/28 late morning / early afternoon.

Final centered US Tarawa casualties:
- KIA/MIA: about 550-700;
- WIA: about 1,350-1,650;
- total: about 1,900-2,300.

The first wave is not easier than historical Tarawa. The lower total loss comes from improved later-wave logistics caused by the different tide date and measured-lane handling.

## 6. 11/26 carrier battle

Japan commits Shokaku, Zuikaku, Hiryu and Soryu after obtaining a fresh valuable contact on a northern US fast-carrier TG. Secondary carriers remain rear/regeneration decks rather than being casually added as equal strike carriers.

Corrected late-1943 aircraft state matters:
- Japan retains far more experienced carrier-air leadership than historical late 1943 because the historical Solomon/Guadalcanal attrition chain is absent;
- D4Y and B6N are materially more mature than historical 11/1943;
- Kinsei Zero is **not zero**: event-local carrier-ready allocation is about 18-22, center 20, after production/acceptance/conversion gates;
- the remainder of the fighter force is late Zuisei Zero;
- US F6F + radar/CIC/fighter-direction + fleet AA remains the stronger integrated air-defense system;
- US new fast-carrier groups have useful Midway-suppression/Wake raid experience but not a prior equal-carrier fleet battle; voided 1943 second-Midway experience is not credited and historical Bunker Hill Rabaul combat is not imported.

Centered first Japanese strike is about 160-165 aircraft. It mission-kills Lexington with one torpedo and 1-2 bomb hits, temporarily damages Yorktown, and leaves Cowpens without major damage.

The US counterstrike, roughly 135-150 aircraft from task groups that can physically reach the contact, mission-kills Soryu with two bomb hits to flight-deck/hangar areas. Shokaku/Zuikaku/Hiryu remain operational.

The corrected Kinsei/F6F-3 microcombat now bounds the magnitude rather than merely the direction. Under the actual 13 Kinsei / 35 late-Zuisei first-strike escort mix, a longer mission-window diagnostic saves roughly one Japanese escort fighter and costs roughly half an additional F6F relative to an all-Zuisei assumption. The 7-Kinsei defensive-CAP component changes fighter losses by less than one aircraft. US CIC/initial-height geometry remains the stronger variable.

Centered irrecoverable aircraft bands therefore remain:
- Japan: 60-75; working center about 67;
- United States: 40-50; working center about 44.

The broad bands are unchanged because the corrected micro-effect was already inside them. The hit-state thresholds were nevertheless rerun: Lexington mission-kill, Yorktown temporary damage, Cowpens no major damage, Soryu mission-kill and Shokaku/Zuikaku/Hiryu operational all remain centered. The exchange still hurts Japan more in experienced strike leaders, navigators and multi-seat crews. Both sides disengage instead of forcing a second all-out strike.

## 7. Damage evacuation, secondary carriers and regeneration

Soryu remains able to make useful speed and withdraws under cover before separating for Truk/Akashi emergency repair and later home-yard work. Her airborne survivors cross-deck temporarily to the three healthy fleet carriers and are then ferried/reassigned to secondary carriers/rear pools.

Junyo/Hiyo/Ryujo/Zuiho now become operationally important as **shock absorbers**: they accept orphan aircraft, ferry replacements, provide rear CAP/training and prevent the three healthy first-line decks from remaining overfull. They do not replace the lost experienced strike cadre.

Independence and Lexington use separate US retreat/repair paths rather than forming one slow wounded convoy. US orphan aircraft are cross-decked or ferried to land bases, exploiting the much deeper US logistics/replacement system.

## 10. Immediate early-December aftermath

Nauru repairs enough runway/handling capacity by early December for limited night staging, not a restored first-line mass air base.

The historical 12/4 Kwajalein/Roi mass carrier raid is not current history. Both sides have learned from GALVANIC:

- the US knows three Japanese first-line carriers plus secondary-carrier depth remain and therefore refuses to commit every deck to a deep fixed-base strike;
- Japan knows F6F/CIC/AA can consume an experienced strike rapidly and will not repeat an all-out carrier attack without a very high-quality positive-EV contact.

Centered early-December US action is a limited outer-Marshalls raid/reconnaissance against Wotje/Maloelap/Mili while a large carrier cover reserve remains against possible Japanese fleet-carrier intervention. Japan disperses/camouflages aircraft and stages a smaller multi-wave night response. No major carrier hit is centered.

Shokaku/Zuikaku/Hiryu may advance as a hidden fleet-in-being behind the Marshalls, but do not launch merely because US carriers are present. Their presence itself forces US CAP/cover/search allocation.

## 11. Strategic closeout

The United States wins GALVANIC strategically: Tarawa and Makin are captured and the Central-Pacific line advances.

The price is substantially higher in fast-carrier terms than historical: Independence and Lexington are long repair casualties, Yorktown is temporarily impaired, Franks is lost and Pierce is damaged. Liscome Bay and Cowpens survive.

Japan fails to save the Gilberts, loses significant Marshall aircraft and an expensive carrier-air cadre, and sends Soryu to repair. However, Shokaku/Zuikaku/Hiryu remain operational, Soryu survives, secondary-carrier depth remains intact and the Marshall network remains materially dangerous.

The correct characterization is not Japanese victory. It is a US strategic victory bought at a price that leaves Japan a meaningful fleet-in-being and a much thicker carrier-air system than historical late-1943 Japan.
