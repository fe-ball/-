## 1. Fighter conclusion

The re-profiled November-1943 microcombat does **not** support an all-purpose conclusion that Kinsei Zero equals or exceeds F6F-3. At equal geometry F6F-3 remains favored, especially as altitude rises. Kinsei materially narrows the gap and, unlike late Zuisei, can exploit a roughly +1,000 m initial height advantage strongly enough to reverse local pairwise odds in the 5-km diagnostic.

The dominant variable is therefore the system that produces initial energy state: warning, ESM/radar cueing, visual confirmation, fighter direction, radio discipline, section leadership and CAP placement. US CIC/fighter direction remains a larger system-level advantage than the Japanese airframe substitution.

## 2. Kinsei versus late Zuisei

Kinsei advantages observed/confirmed:
- better climb and closure against F6F;
- better ability to rejoin an escort after a high-speed defensive pass;
- heavier 20-mm + 13.2-mm battery and better protection;
- much better conversion of an initial altitude advantage into first-shot/kill opportunity;
- lower fighter attrition than late Zuisei under the same F6F/CIC geometry.

Late Zuisei advantages retained:
- longer safe escort radius;
- lower carrier approach speed and lower small-deck recovery burden;
- mature spares, maintenance, deck routines and pilot conversion;
- better fit for long-duration search/escort and secondary/small-carrier operations.

Actual GALVANIC mix matters. 13 Kinsei among 48 first-strike escorts improves Japanese fighter loss by only about one aircraft in the longer mission-window diagnostic versus an all-Zuisei force. Seven Kinsei in the defensive CAP changes losses by less than one aircraft. A hypothetical all-Kinsei force would improve exchange substantially, but is not date-gated as available.

## 3. Deployment doctrine

Centered post-GALVANIC policy:
- Shokaku / Zuikaku: Kinsei priority as production and qualified pilots permit;
- Hiryu: mixed fighter group;
- Junyo / Hiyo: conditional mixed group when F6F-contact/interceptor duty justifies higher landing/deck burden;
- Zuiho-class and Ryujo: late-Zuisei-heavy unless deck trials prove a specific Kinsei detachment safe and worthwhile;
- do not strip long-range escort/search capacity merely to maximize headline fighter speed.

## 4. F6F/CIC lesson

The F6F is dangerous not only because of aircraft performance. Radar/CIC/fighter direction repeatedly produces the more important advantage: initial altitude and first-shot geometry. Japanese development priorities after GALVANIC therefore give high value to:
- earlier passive warning and bearing history;
- rapid cue-to-visual-confirmation cycle;
- reliable VHF/radio procedure and section control;
- layered CAP altitude assignments;
- disciplined refusal to chase decoys or low-value contacts;
- preserving energy for escort/rejoin rather than prolonged turning fights.

“Faster Zero” without this soft system does not solve the problem.

## 5. B6N / D4Y and strike-cadre lesson

B6N and D4Y maturity helps route selection, closure, formation and weapon-release probability, but the US F6F/CIC/AA system still imposes heavy losses. The binding Japanese post-battle debt is not raw airframe quantity. It is experienced torpedo leaders, long-range navigators, dive-bombing leaders and trained multi-seat crews lost over the US formation and therefore poorly recoverable.

Secondary carriers and shore/rear units can absorb orphan aircraft and provide replacement decks. They cannot instantly recreate this cadre.

## 6. Quantitative GALVANIC correction

Revalidated broad irrecoverable-aircraft bands remain:
- Japan 60-75; working center about 67;
- United States 40-50; working center about 44.

These centers are macro adjudication points inside pre-existing bands, not direct microcombat outputs. The Kinsei correction is measured in a few fighters/crew, not a new carrier-hit sequence. Lexington and Soryu mission-kill results remain centered after threshold recheck.
