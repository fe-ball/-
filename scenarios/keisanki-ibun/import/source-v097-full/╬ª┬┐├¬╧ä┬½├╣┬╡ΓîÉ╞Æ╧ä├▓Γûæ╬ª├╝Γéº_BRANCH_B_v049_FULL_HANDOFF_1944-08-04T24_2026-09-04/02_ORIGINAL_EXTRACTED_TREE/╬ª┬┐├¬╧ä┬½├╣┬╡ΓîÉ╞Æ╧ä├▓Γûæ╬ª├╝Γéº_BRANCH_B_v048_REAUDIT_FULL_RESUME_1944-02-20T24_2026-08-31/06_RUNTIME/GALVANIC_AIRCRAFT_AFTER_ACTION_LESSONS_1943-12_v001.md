# GALVANIC Japanese aircraft after-action lessons, December 1943 v001

status: AUTHORITATIVE_BRANCH_AFTER_ACTION_LESSON
scope: carrier / land-based aircraft employment after Branch-B GALVANIC

## 1. Why this lesson exists

The final GALVANIC re-audit corrects an event-local fielding error. The 1943-10-01 aircraft ledger was valid in refusing to assume a combat-ready Kinsei-Zero unit at that cutoff, but it was too easy for downstream execution to read that as "Kinsei Zero remains zero through late November." The HARD aircraft source instead has pilot production beginning in September 1943 (centers: Sep 6, Oct 12, Nov 24). Production is not readiness, but by 1943-11-26 a small operational conversion pool is unavoidable.

Event-local centered combat use is therefore 18-22 Kinsei Zero, center 20, concentrated mainly on Shokaku/Zuikaku with smaller numbers on Hiryu/Soryu. The rest of raw production remains in acceptance, conversion, training, rear allocation or maintenance and is not spawned into combat.

## 2. Kinsei Zero versus late Zuisei Zero: what GALVANIC actually teaches

The battle does **not** show that the Kinsei Zero has made the F6F system obsolete. It shows that the Japanese carrier fighter force now has two useful tools with different optimum missions.

### Kinsei Zero strengths observed

- Unit-median speed around 586-593 km/h and 6,000 m climb around 6:35-6:55 materially improve intercept closure, rejoin after a defensive pass and the ability to disengage from a bad low/medium-altitude geometry.
- 20 mm x2 + 13.2 mm x2 and additional protection give a better opening-fire and survival package than the late Zuisei airframe.
- Against F6F, the aircraft restores **tactical choice** in some low/medium-altitude situations. Escorts do not have to accept every high-energy US pass and then fail to reform.
- In GALVANIC, a dozen-plus Kinsei escorts and a handful of Kinsei CAP aircraft modestly increase the number of Japanese attack aircraft reaching weapon release and reduce Japanese fighter losses compared with an all-Zuisei assumption.

### Kinsei Zero limitations observed

- F6F still owns the stronger high-speed dive, protection/firepower balance, and—most importantly—the US radar/CIC/VHF fighter-direction system. A better Japanese individual aircraft is not a substitute for that system.
- Safe long-range escort is only about 550-620 km versus about 650-750 km for the late Zuisei Zero. The Kinsei conversion therefore cannot simply replace Zuisei on every long-distance carrier escort mission.
- Carrier approach is roughly 150-157 km/h versus 135-145 km/h for Zuisei. Shokaku/Zuikaku can absorb this; Hiryu can use it with a somewhat worse deck cycle; Junyo/Hiyo are workable with tighter wind/deck margins; small/light decks should remain mixed or Zuisei-heavy.
- Early conversion procedures, deck handling and spares are less mature even though the Kinsei engine family itself is comparatively mature. The battle does not justify assuming identical sortie-generation maturity to the long-established Zuisei fleet.

## 3. Resulting fighter allocation doctrine

Do **not** order an all-Kinsei conversion.

Centered post-GALVANIC policy:

1. Shokaku/Zuikaku: give Kinsei priority for fleet CAP and medium-range escort against F6F-class opposition.
2. Hiryu: mixed force; use Kinsei where closure/climb/firepower matters, but preserve Zuisei for long escort and deck-cycle flexibility.
3. Junyo/Hiyo: mixed force with weather/wind/deck gate; Kinsei useful but not allowed to choke recovery throughput.
4. Zuiho/Shoho/Ryuho-class decks: remain predominantly Zuisei, with only a limited Kinsei fraction where deck conditions justify it.
5. Ryujo: Zuisei remains the principal fighter.
6. Long-range escort and sustained deck operations: retain Zuisei as a first-choice type rather than treating it as obsolete.

In short: **Kinsei is the better F6F-contact fighter; Zuisei is the better long-leg / light-deck / high-sortie-rate carrier fighter.** Mixed fitness beats wholesale replacement.

## 4. B6N and D4Y lesson

Branch-advanced B6N/D4Y maturity contributes directly to the 11/26 strike reaching release geometry. Higher speed, range and formation/rejoin performance matter.

But GALVANIC also demonstrates their hard limit: once a strike is detected early and forced through disciplined F6F interception plus dense fleet AA, neither speed nor better attack geometry provides protection. The irreplaceable losses are not just airframes; they are section leaders, long-range navigators and trained multi-seat attack crews.

Therefore:

- preserve experienced torpedo/navigator cadres and rotate them into conversion/training units after high-loss strikes;
- use D4Y speed for reconnaissance/recontact and selected attack tasks rather than treating every D4Y as expendable dive-bomber mass;
- B6N expansion remains justified, but sortie generation must be tied to trained crew and spares, not factory totals;
- never infer that a second 160-aircraft strike has the same quality merely because the airframes can be replenished.

## 5. Operational-system lesson

The largest US advantage remains the **system**: radar warning -> CIC/fighter direction -> F6F interception -> layered fleet AA -> rescue/repair/replacement.

The Japanese response is therefore not "build a slightly faster Zero and repeat 1941." It is:

- mixed Kinsei/Zuisei role allocation;
- better passive warning and search cueing;
- disciplined CAP altitude/sector allocation;
- attack only on fresh, valuable contacts;
- avoid repeated all-out strikes after the experienced strike cadre has been spent;
- keep secondary-carrier decks as regeneration/cross-deck shock absorbers.

GALVANIC strengthens the 1944 Kinsei production/training case, but does not conjure additional engines, carrier-qualified pilots or A7M production ahead of their clocks.
