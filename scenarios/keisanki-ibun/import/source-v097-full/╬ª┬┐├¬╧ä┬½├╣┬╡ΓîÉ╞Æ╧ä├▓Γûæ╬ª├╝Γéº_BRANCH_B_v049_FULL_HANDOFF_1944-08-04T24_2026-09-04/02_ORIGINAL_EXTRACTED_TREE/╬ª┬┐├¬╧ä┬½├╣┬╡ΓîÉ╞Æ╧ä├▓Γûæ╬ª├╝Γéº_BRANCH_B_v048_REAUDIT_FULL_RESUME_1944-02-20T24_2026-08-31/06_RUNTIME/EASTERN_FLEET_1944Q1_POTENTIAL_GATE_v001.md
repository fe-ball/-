## 1. What Britain can plausibly send east

Historical late-Dec 1943/Jan 1944 movement demonstrates that Britain can transfer a heavy group on the order of Queen Elizabeth, Valiant, Renown, Illustrious and Unicorn without abandoning the European war. Branch B accepts this as a plausible year-end eastbound baseline; exact January arrival/readiness/contact is a future simulation event.

Branch differences:
- Formidable and Indomitable are permanently lost.
- Saratoga cannot later be loaned to Eastern Fleet; she was lost in Branch Midway.
- Illustrious is available but carries deferred modernization debt.
- Victorious' US loan/return/refit clock is displaced later than historical.
- destroyer/escort reserve elasticity is somewhat worse than historical.

Therefore the likely 1944H1 Eastern Fleet is **surface-heavy, carrier-thin, escort-constrained**.

## 2. British trade preferences against Japan

- Illustrious vs Fuso/Yamashiro/Ise/Hyuga: unacceptable trade. The only modern flight deck is locally more valuable than one Japanese old battleship.
- Queen Elizabeth/Valiant vs one Japanese old battleship: possible loss to accept if the Japanese ship is very likely destroyed and operational objective is important, but not a deliberately sought 1:1 exchange.
- cruiser + limited destroyer damage for a Japanese battleship: attractive if geometry is favorable.
- repeated DD-for-DD attrition: unattractive because British destroyers also service Atlantic/Arctic/Med/Indian Ocean convoy requirements.

The preferred method is deterrence by heavy ships plus asymmetric attrition by submarines, mines, land-based reconnaissance/air and selective night light-force action.

## 3. Japanese observation of the closing window

Japan should understand that the period before Britain closes the Italian/Med naval problem is the window in which old Japanese battleships can operate unusually freely in the Bay of Bengal. The 1943 Chittagong campaign therefore has a real time premium: secure the logistics root before British heavy units can return east.

A large British movement via Britain -> Mediterranean/Suez -> Red Sea/Aden -> Ceylon is difficult to hide completely. Axis reporting, traffic analysis, port observations and later Japanese reconnaissance can raise confidence in stages. Japan is unlikely to learn only when British battleships appear over the horizon.

Operational implication after the movement is recognized: Fuso/Yamashiro/Ise/Hyuga move from near-routine rotating shore-artillery duty toward sortie reserve / conditional bombardment. Predictable weekly exposure off Chittagong becomes too dangerous.

## 4. German submarine coordination

The British heavy return makes Japanese-German Indian Ocean submarine coordination more attractive. Rational division of labor is sectoral, not a unified tactical command: Japanese boats emphasize Bay of Bengal/Andaman/Ceylon approaches and fleet intelligence/attack opportunities; German Monsun boats can work farther west/south/Arabian Sea/Ceylon western approaches as availability permits.

German U-boat strength and survivability are worsening by 1944, so this is an opportunity multiplier, not a bottomless reinforcement pool. Do not count German boats before tracing actual transit, readiness and patrol days.

## 5. Mandatory next calculation

Before moving the date into Jan 1944, build Japan's actual naval wallet:
1. fleet destroyers: total/ready, Pacific mandatory cover, Indian Ocean transferable band;
2. submarines: conventional ocean boats, six confirmed high-speed-submerged boats, patrol-day commitments and transfer times;
3. heavy/light cruisers: total/ready, Pacific tasks, whether British slow-battleship arrival justifies a westward reinforcement;
4. old battleships: Fuso/Yamashiro/Ise/Hyuga forward/rear rotation and withdrawal basing;
5. German boats: actual Monsun/Indian Ocean availability, not nominal U-boat totals.

Use origin -> readiness -> route -> fuel/escort -> arrival -> local support. No unit spawning or fleet teleportation.
