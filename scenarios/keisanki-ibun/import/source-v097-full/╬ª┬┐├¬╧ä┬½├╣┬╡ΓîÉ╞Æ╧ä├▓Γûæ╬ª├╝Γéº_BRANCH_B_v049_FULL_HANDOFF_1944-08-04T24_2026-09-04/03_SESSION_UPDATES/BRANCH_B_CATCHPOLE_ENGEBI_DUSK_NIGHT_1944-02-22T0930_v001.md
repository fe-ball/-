## 1. Sunset-crossing adjudication

The Branch mandatory sunset rule is triggered.

- Historical Engebi could be declared secured in mid-afternoon while mopping continued well afterward; therefore “organized resistance broken”, “island operationally secure”, and “last armed pocket eliminated” are kept separate.
- Branch Engebi has materially more post-suppression mortar/MG recovery, better local ammunition survival, and somewhat better local C2 than the historical island, but no Peleliu-style geometric depth.
- From the 12:30 state of roughly 300–410 immediately coherent local fighters, U.S. tank/engineer/direct-fire reduction is sufficient to destroy the island-wide defense during the afternoon, but not to eliminate every mutually supporting local cell before sunset.
- Working organized-resistance break band: **18:05–19:05**, center about **18:35**.
- Local sunset is treated as an astronomical gate, not as a fabricated exact operational-clock number; the centerline has the last coherent Japanese resistance persisting roughly **10–35 minutes into darkness**.
- Operational control of the airfield, beaches, and main movement routes is already U.S. by then. The night gate therefore creates infiltration/mop-up effects, not a second defensive line.

## 5. 21/22 Feb night action

### Japanese choice

Because:
- the island cannot be held conventionally,
- no meaningful reinforcement is possible,
- beach exits are U.S.-controlled,
- local C2 is fragmented,

the centered Japanese night behavior is **not** a single mass counterattack.

Instead:
- **3–5 infiltration/local-attack packets** operate over the night;
- total participants: roughly **55–90**, not simultaneous;
- largest packet: roughly **20–35**;
- tasks include probing Marine seams, attacking aid/supply/vehicle areas if reachable, recovering ammunition and wounded, and attempting to move between surviving pockets.

### U.S. response

- company/battalion perimeters tighten before darkness;
- pre-registered artillery and mortar defensive fires are retained but used carefully because friendly units cover much of the island;
- star shell/illumination, MG final protective fires, tanks held near cleared lanes, and short direct-fire missions dominate;
- reserve movement is deliberately limited to avoid friendly-fire confusion.

### Night tactical result

Centered:
- 1–2 Japanese packets penetrate roughly **30–80 m** into a Marine perimeter/support lane before being stopped;
- local disruption lasts roughly **10–30 minutes**;
- no battalion CP, artillery battery, beach exit, airfield control point, or major supply dump is overrun;
- **0–1 light vehicle/LVT repairably damaged** by close attack/demolition;
- no irrecoverable M4 loss.

Night losses:
- U.S. combat casualties: **22–38**, including roughly **7–12 killed/missing**;
- Japanese killed/irrecoverable: **45–75**;
- captured during/after failed penetrations: **5–12**, largely wounded/exhausted personnel.

## 9. Operational consequences

The night matters even though it cannot save Engebi.

### U.S. force availability
- 3/22 elements, tank/engineer teams and supporting fires remain tied to Engebi overnight.
- They are **not** available on 22 Feb morning as fully rested, fully serviced reserve assets.
- 1/22 and 2/22 are materially fatigued and require reorganization, casualty replacement at key billets, ammunition replenishment and vehicle servicing.

### Intelligence clock
- Important documents, command-post material and prisoners from the last central-grove cells are recovered later than in a clean afternoon closeout.
- Translation/interrogation throughput therefore extends into the night and early 22 Feb.
- The next-island target decision must not assume that all Engebi-derived intelligence was already available on the afternoon of 21 Feb.

### Schedule
- This does not automatically cancel a 22 Feb assault elsewhere.
- It does reduce the quality of the Marine reserve/tank wallet available early on 22 Feb and creates a real trade between attacking promptly with the 106th Infantry and waiting for stronger Marine/tank support.
