## Recent strategic-audit conclusions to preserve
### Allied cumulative-cost method
For every loss/base/campaign, track:
1. physical loss;
2. service lost;
3. maintenance expenditure no longer required;
4. substitution cost;
5. opportunity cost of the substitute;
6. time/degradation interest until substitution;
7. enemy use of captured assets/location.

A base loss can reduce bulk logistics while increasing scarce-resource burden. Midway is the template: the US no longer feeds/builds the base, but loses search/submarine-forward services and must pay containment/suppression with PBY/submarines/escort/deck cycles.

### Carrier/major-unit value
Do not value a major ship only by sorties actually flown.
Separate:
- current combat power;
- friendly **option value / planning confidence** (“because Yamato / a carrier exists in harbor, plans can be written around the possibility of using it”);
- enemy fleet-in-being constraint;
- replacement-infrastructure avoidance value.

Carrier loss is especially expensive because land bases reproduce its services only through duplicated fixed infrastructure across uncertain geography: runways, AVGAS, ammunition, radar, AA, repair, spares, trucks, engineers, etc. In 1942 Pacific, loss of mobile carrier reserve therefore drives repeated fortress investment at PM/Santo/NC/Fiji/Tonga/Samoa/Australia and raises shipping/engineer/tanker demand.
