## 1. 1 October Combined Fleet demand

Combined Fleet requests a force large enough to achieve two simultaneous purposes:

1. seize the Noumea–Tontouta southern strategic complex;
2. force or punish a U.S. fleet intervention under Santo-based air/search/submarine support.

The demand is deliberately larger than the force that must be committed on D-day.
A reserve is required because Santo proved that:
- a carrier mission kill does not destroy its air group;
- Washington-class radar battleships remain dangerous without U.S. carriers;
- destroyer and cruiser screens are consumed quickly in repeated night actions;
- tankers and reload/ammunition state limit how long nominal hull superiority can remain on station.

## 4. Carrier doctrine after Santo

Main fleet carriers are NOT used for daily fixed-base harassment.

Santo land air takes:
- broad search;
- Efate/Tontouta fixed-target pressure;
- contact maintenance;
- local fighter staging.

Support carriers take:
- convoy CAP;
- ASW;
- local strike;
- rescue/recovery.

Fleet carriers preserve:
- fleet strike;
- fleet CAP;
- second strike;
- cross-deck reserve.

This is the direct Santo lesson.

### Combined Fleet preference
Yamamoto/Combined Fleet **wants Yamato + Mutsu available as a distant decisive-battle backstop** if intelligence shows Washington + South Dakota + North Carolina concentrating.

But this is conditional, not automatic:
- these ships consume tanker capacity at a very high rate;
- Santo is not a heavy-fleet logistics base;
- exposing Yamato near Santo also creates a submarine/air intelligence target;
- their value is greatest in open-water battle, not routine shore bombardment.

Center policy:
- prepare Yamato/Mutsu to sortie;
- move them south only when U.S. fast-battleship concentration is confirmed or strongly indicated;
- never count their 18/16-inch gunfire into routine D-day beach bombardment.

## 11. Tankers / oilers — the true hard gate

Without the Yamato/Mutsu reserve:
- center operational tanker/support requirement for carrier, fast-BB and assault cycling: **5–7 oilers/tankers**.

With Yamato + Mutsu moved into the southern decisive-battle system:
- requirement rises to roughly **7–9** tanker/support hulls or equivalent fuel capacity/turnaround.

This is not all alongside the battle fleet simultaneously.
It includes:
- fleet replenishment group;
- assault fuel support;
- Santo staging stock replenishment;
- reserve tanker rotation.

Operational consequence:
- a one-oiler mission kill can still cancel a second bombardment night or shorten carrier station time.
- a two-oiler disruption can force the heavy reserve to remain north even if Yamato/Mutsu are nominally ready.

Therefore tanker state is a direct gate on "decisive battle doctrine".

## 15. Final planning judgment

The Japanese November force is stronger than the old New Caledonia settlement in **system integration**, not because every hull is magically pristine.

Santo creates:
- forward fighter/search support;
- shorter fleet/convoy transit;
- better aircraft recovery;
- a southern tanker/anchorage node.

Santo also costs:
- carrier aircrew;
- Kongo readiness;
- several destroyer work-up cycles;
- transport and assault-equipment depth.

Therefore the 8 November Japanese advantage is best described as:

**more decks, more surviving hulls, better battlefield geometry, and better learned doctrine — but with uneven air-group quality and a tanker-dependent heavy-battle-line option.**

The central Combined Fleet temptation is to turn that geometry into one more fleet decision before U.S. carrier construction catches up.
The central Naval Staff restraint is to take Noumea/Tontouta and then stop rather than roll automatically into Fiji/Samoa.
