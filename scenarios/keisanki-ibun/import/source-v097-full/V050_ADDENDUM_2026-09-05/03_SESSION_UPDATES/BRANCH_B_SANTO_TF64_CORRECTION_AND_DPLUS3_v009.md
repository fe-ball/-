## 1. Why a retroactive correction is required

Historical U.S. disposition provides a hard timing anchor:
- on 23 September 1942 San Francisco, Salt Lake City, Boise, Helena, Minneapolis, Chester and Destroyer Squadron 12 were organized as TF 64;
- on 24 September the force headed for the New Hebrides.

Noumea–Espiritu Santo is only about 415 nautical miles direct. Even allowing formation routing and operational caution, a cruiser force ordered north on 24 September can be in the Santo operational area by 25–26 September.

Branch-specific factors make a late arrival less, not more, likely:
- Hornet is already lost at modified MI, so no Hornet carrier screen competes for the same cruisers.
- the historical Guadalcanal reinforcement chain is absent.
- Santo itself is the threatened major base, giving COMSOPAC a direct reason to concentrate surface units.
- MI losses still apply: Astoria, Pensacola and Northampton are not available; Benham is sunk.
- Portland/Vincennes are excluded from this correction because their alternate MI repair dates are still an open gate.

Therefore the earlier assumption that only Washington/Helena/Atlanta/Quincy-level local surface power is available through 25–27 September is too weak.

## 3. Corrected 25 Sep D-Day night action

The Japanese Hiei/Kirishima bombardment plan detects a materially stronger U.S. surface concentration than assumed in v006.

### Japanese adaptation
- keep the destroyer torpedo screen well forward;
- battleships bombard from a more conservative geometry;
- break off earlier once Washington and multiple cruiser contacts become clear;
- do not seek a battleship duel.

### U.S. adaptation
- Washington stays behind/inside the cruiser-DD screen rather than leading a close pursuit.
- Helena/Boise/Minneapolis/Chester add substantial medium/heavy gun volume against the Japanese forward screen.
- U.S. formation complexity prevents all ships from exploiting Washington's radar picture equally.

### Center result
Japanese first/second torpedo waves:
- Washington avoids.
- one U.S. destroyer, **Duncan**, takes a Type 93 hit and is lost/abandoned.
- no center U.S. cruiser torpedo hit on this night.

Japanese damage:
- **Murasame**: medium-heavy damage, campaign surface-action mission kill; survives.
- one additional Japanese destroyer: light damage.
- no center Japanese sinking.

Battleship gun action:
- no center 16-inch hit on Hiei/Kirishima.
- repeated Washington straddles/near misses force defensive maneuver.

Corrected shore bombardment:
- Hiei + Kirishima combined 14-inch expenditure: ~140–190 rounds, center ~165.
- limited cruiser fire additional.
- this is materially below v006's ~250 rounds.

Operational verdict:
- U.S. stronger surface reinforcement reduces Japanese fire-support delivery.
- Japanese Type 93 screen still prevents a clean Washington pursuit.
- surface action is tactically inconclusive; shore-fire denial favors the U.S.

### Dawn 26 Sep correction
Santo immediate sortie-generation:
- ~60–70%, center ~65%
instead of v006/v007 center ~52%.

# 6. D+3 — 28 September 1942

## 6.1 U.S. objective

COMSOPAC no longer attempts to save Santo as a functioning base.

Priority:
1. hold the Luganville–Segond perimeter until dark;
2. destroy fuel, workshops, repair equipment and classified material that cannot be removed;
3. evacuate the bulk of the remaining combat force on the night of 28/29 Sep;
4. leave only a controlled rear guard, not a sacrificial garrison.

## 6.2 Japanese objective

1. place the Segond embarkation area under observed artillery fire before night;
2. seize Luganville road junctions and cut embarkation routes;
3. prevent a clean final evacuation;
4. avoid losing major surface units merely to enter a mined harbor covered by Washington.

- shortened perimeter;
- base AA and surviving artillery used in direct/ground roles;

Japanese strengths:

- mature artillery/infantry coordination relative to historical Japanese median;
