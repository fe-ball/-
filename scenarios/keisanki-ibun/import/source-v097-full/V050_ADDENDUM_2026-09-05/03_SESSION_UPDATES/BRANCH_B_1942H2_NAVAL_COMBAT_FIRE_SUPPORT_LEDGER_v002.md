## 3. Shore bombardment / counter-bombardment ledger

### 3.1 Historical physical anchor

Kongo + Haruna historically fired almost 1,000 14-inch shells into Henderson Field in one night, destroying more than half the aircraft and damaging most of the remainder. This proves the destructive ceiling against fixed airfield/fuel/communications targets when the bombardment group is allowed to sit on target.

It does NOT mean:
- Kongo-class has that hit rate against maneuvering warships;
- two battleships can repeat a ~1,000-shell mission every night;
- ships loaded/configured for shore bombardment suffer no penalty if suddenly caught in surface action.

### 3.2 Branch working fire-support bands — adjudication parameters, not historical facts

Santo, one Kongo-pair:
- planned pre-landing heavy mission: ~300–600 x 14-in shells TOTAL across the pair, center ~450, plus cruiser 8-in fire;
- if U.S. surface threat is confidently suppressed, an exceptional surge toward the historical ~1,000-shell ceiling is physically possible but not center;
- later short support: ~80–180 x 14-in shells total per pair per night, normally only 1–2 additional nights before fuel/ammo/exposure costs force rotation.

New Caledonia:
- because U.S. fast-BB threat is larger, use four surviving Kongo hulls as a rotation system rather than one permanent bombardment pair:
  - pair A: shore-fire window;
  - pair B: night interception / carrier-heavy cover;
  - rotate after ammunition/fuel/damage state.
- a Santo-damaged Kongo hull reduces this system directly; do not reset four healthy ships in Nov.

Ammunition-state rule:
- track shore-bombardment ammunition and surface-action readiness separately.
- a group prepared with shore ammunition / fire-control setup may enter a sudden surface action at a tactical disadvantage; historical Kondo experience is the physical warning.

### 3.3 U.S. defensive fire support

Fast U.S. battleships can deliver powerful counter-bombardment, but their highest-value mission in a Japanese carrier-dominated amphibious zone may be night interdiction rather than predictable coastal station-keeping.

Working rule:
- U.S. BB/cruiser shore-fire missions are short, night-biased, and conditional on avoiding Japanese carrier dawn search and Long-Lance surface screens.
- local U.S. ground observers make fire on known Japanese beachheads/roads potentially effective.
- every night a U.S. fast BB spends on shore fire is a night it may be less optimally placed for transport interception, and vice versa.

## 4. Damage-state calibration for sea/shore linkage

### 4.1 Kongo-class vs U.S. 16-inch

Do not use sink/no-sink only.
Working state transition:
- 1 solid 16-in hit: sinking still unlikely, but meaningful probability of fire-control, machinery, turret, flooding or mission damage; shore-fire schedule may be lost.
- 2 solid 16-in hits: campaign withdrawal / medium-heavy damage is a strong center, not automatic sinking.
Old Branch Haruna 2-hit outcome is a useful calibration but is NOT automatically copied into the new battle.

### 4.2 U.S. fast BB vs Japanese torpedo

One Long-Lance/submarine torpedo is not automatic battleship sinking.
Historical North Carolina could still make 25 knots after one submarine torpedo hit, but the damage removed her for repair.
Therefore use:
- survival probability high after a single non-magazine hit;
- campaign mission-kill probability substantial;
- multiple hits or unlucky location can escalate sharply.

### 4.3 Cruisers / destroyers

Radar first contact does not erase Type 93 danger.
A U.S. cruiser line that opens fire can still expose bearing and geometry for a devastating Japanese torpedo counter-salvo.
Conversely Japanese optical/torpedo skill does not negate SG-enabled early detection and heavy first gunfire.

### 4.4 Oilers / transports

AO/large tanker hits have outsized operational effect.
A tanker need not sink to:
- shorten carrier on-station time;
- cancel a second bombardment night;
- force a battleship group out of the box;
- delay second-echelon unloading.

Hence sea battles must track tanker mission-kill separately from merchant-tonnage loss.

## 6. Sea-to-ground coupling rules for Santo and New Caledonia

Every naval result changes land combat immediately.

1. Japanese BB/CA damage
   -> fewer bombardment nights
   -> U.S. 105/155mm survival and firing density rise
   -> Japanese D/D+1 beach casualties rise; unloading may slow.

2. U.S. BB damage/withdrawal
   -> Japanese bombardment windows widen
   -> transports can remain closer/longer at night
   -> U.S. ground counterattack loses naval interdiction support.

3. Wasp damage/air-group attrition
   -> fewer daylight transport strikes/CAP distractions
   -> Japanese second echelon arrives earlier/less disrupted.

4. Japanese carrier-air loss
   -> weaker CAP and fewer fixed-target attacks
   -> U.S. base aviation survives at higher sortie density.

5. DD loss/damage
   -> not merely one hull loss; it subtracts ASW, AA, night screen, rescue and transport-cover slots simultaneously.

6. Oiler mission-kill
   -> shorten heavy-group/carrier time-on-station BEFORE converting it to arbitrary sunk-tonnage effects.

7. Commander casualties/experience
   -> carry into later doctrine and formation quality.

### 10.7 Geography/contact correction

Santo is not Ironbottom Sound.
- Segond Channel/Pallikulo/Turtle Bay create local choke points for transports and shore support, but the surrounding sea is open enough for heavy groups to maneuver and decline contact.
- U.S. radar/night advantage therefore raises interception probability; it does NOT force a surface battle every night.
- Japanese optical/Type 93 strength matters most if U.S. forces close the transport/bombardment box or if contact geometry is created by reconnaissance/submarine reports.

Thus Santo sea combat should be modeled as a 7–9 day campaign of:
1) search/submarine shaping,
2) carrier/base-air attacks,
3) one or more attempted night interceptions,
4) shore-bombardment windows,
5) transport/oiler survival and unloading,
not as one deterministic 'Santo Night Battle'.
